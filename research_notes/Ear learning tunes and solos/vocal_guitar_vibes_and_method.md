# Vocal, Single-Line Guitar, and Vibraphone Ear-Learning Targets + First-Transcription Method

Research pass dated 2026-11-02. The learner is an advanced classically trained pianist, new to jazz, who will learn single-line solos by singing them first and then playing them on piano. No notation or transcriptions are reproduced here.

Source-quality note: Most educator material is on blogs (JazzAdvice, Learn Jazz Standards, PianoGroove, KeyboardImprov) or forums (jazzguitar.be, cafesaxophone, visionmusic). Several JazzAdvice pages put full content behind a PRO paywall, so only the free text was readable. Forum claims are anecdotal and are marked as such. Some album/year details come from training knowledge rather than a page fetched in this pass, and those are flagged.

## 1. Specific recordings: singers, single-line guitar, vibraphone

### Takeaway
Only a few recordings in these three categories are named in the sources as beginner or accessible ear-learning targets: Wes Montgomery's "D-Natural Blues", Kenny Burrell's "Chitlins con Carne", Milt Jackson's "Bags' Groove", and some Grant Green tracks. Vocal recordings come up as models for phrasing, not as "first transcription" picks. Ella's Berlin "How High the Moon" and King Pleasure's "Moody's Mood for Love" are the best-documented vocal choices. Holiday/Lester Young 1937–38 and Sinatra are the phrasing models educators cite.

### Inferences
- The best first target for this learner is probably Wes's "D-Natural Blues", Burrell's "Chitlins con Carne", or the first chorus of Milt Jackson's "Bags' Groove". All three are 12-bar blues at a manageable tempo with clear single-line statements, which matches JazzAdvice's criteria in Q3. Guitar and vibes lines also sit easily under a pianist's right hand, since they have no breath-shaped long tones.
- *Ella in Berlin* "How High the Moon" is a great listen but a poor first whole-solo target. It runs many choruses at a brisk tempo with quotes. One chorus, or a few phrases, is a sensible scope.
- "Moody's Mood for Love" fits a sing-first method unusually well. The words carry the rhythm of a real bebop tenor solo, so singing the lyrics is a mnemonic for the instrumental line. That is my reasoning; no source frames it as a pedagogy tool.
- Holiday and Sinatra are better used to learn *melody interpretation* (backphrasing, rhythmic elasticity) than as "solo" targets. Singing a head the way Holiday sang it, then playing it on piano, is a melody-paraphrase exercise, not a transcription of an improvised solo.

## 2. Why instrumentalists should learn from singers

### Takeaway
The standard argument is that knowing a song's lyrics and the way singers phrase them shapes an instrumentalist's interpretation, especially on ballads. This is tied to Lester Young. Sinatra and Holiday are the go-to models for rhythmic elasticity. Primary-source documentation of Young's exact words was not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Young believed a song's lyrics mattered in his improvisations, both when soloing and when accompanying singers such as Billie Holiday. — [EBSCO Research Starter](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/lester-young)
- A Fresh Sound Records album page says Young believed "every jazz player should know the lyrics of the song he plays." It notes that Gene Lees discusses Young's "oft-quoted comment" that saxophonists need to know the lyrics to fully interpret a ballad. This comes from a search snippet; the page redirected and wasn't fully fetched. — [Fresh Sound Records](https://freshsoundrecords.com/don-menza-frank-strazzeri-albums/52222-ballads.html)
- Young's phrasing is described as smooth, melodic, and economical, with unplayed notes as important as played ones. A 1949 *Record Changer* article says boppers studied his tone and phrasing and adapted it to brass. — [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/lester-young); [The Jazz Tome / Record Changer](https://thejazztome.info/glossary/the-parent-style/)
- KeyboardImprov tells pianists to (a) transcribe a singer's melody as actually sung, to see how far it departs from the written rhythm; (b) delay or rush notes for elasticity; (c) know at least the opening lyrics and the song's mood; and (d) learn the verses (examples given: "Stardust", "Autumn Leaves", "Over the Rainbow", "Lush Life"). — [KeyboardImprov](https://keyboardimprov.com/5-things-jazz-pianists-can-learn-from-frank-sinatra/)
- Iverson: "Holiday's chiaroscuro is a strong argument for why jazz players play Tin Pan Alley." — [Ethan Iverson](https://ethaniverson.com/rhythm-and-blues/a-beginners-guide-to-the-master-takes/)
- Vocalese, as in Jefferson and King Pleasure, is literally singing instrumental solos note for note. — [Wikipedia: Eddie Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddie_Jefferson)

### Inferences
- For a sing-first learner, vocal recordings remove the step of translating an instrument's timbre into the voice. Phrase lengths are already breath-sized, which is a natural template for right-hand phrasing on piano.
- Classically trained pianists often play written rhythms literally. Holiday or Sinatra heads are a direct corrective, since KeyboardImprov's main point is that these singers deliberately don't sing the sheet-music rhythm.

### Gaps
- The often-repeated anecdote of Young stopping a ballad solo because he "forgot the words" was not found in any source this pass. It should not be quoted without a primary citation (e.g., Lewis Porter's *Lester Young*, Gene Lees's writings, or Dexter Gordon interviews, where similar stories appear).
- No vocal-jazz educator source (Berklee voice faculty, etc.) addressing instrumentalists was retrieved.

## 3. Method: choosing a first solo

### Takeaway
Educators agree on these points: pick something you love, keep it far smaller and easier than you think (a single line under four bars, a few phrases, or one chorus is fine), use medium or medium-slow 4/4, limited range and rhythmic density, familiar changes (blues or ii-V), and a solo you can sing. No source gives a typical time to finish.

### Cited Findings
- **Size:** For a first selection, "choose a short line, anything under four measures, over a chord progression that is familiar to you". "make it 10 times easier than you think you need it to be." — [JazzAdvice, Getting Started With Transcription](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/getting-started-with-transcription/)
- **Scope:** "A few choruses, one chorus, or even eight bars is sufficient." — [JazzAdvice, What, Why, Where, Who, When, and How](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/what-why-where-who-when-and-how-to-transcribe/). "You don't need the whole solo"; a chorus or a few well-learned lines is enough. — [JazzAdvice, 3 Secrets](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/the-3-secrets-to-transcribing-jazz-solos/)
- **Simplicity:** Avoid solos with too many notes, fast tempos, or extreme ranges. Choose material you can make progress on in one session, and choose a solo you can sing. — [JazzAdvice, 3 Secrets](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/the-3-secrets-to-transcribing-jazz-solos/)
- **Blues criteria:** basic 4/4 at medium or medium-slow tempo; a mix of rhythmically decisive "blues statements" (mostly quarter notes and longer) and eighth-note lines; clear phrasing; standard changes; emotional impact ("make sure you work on blues solos that have an emotional impact on you"). — [JazzAdvice, 8 Blues Solos](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/8-awesome-blues-solos-for-beginners-to-transcribe/)
- **Love it:** "I try to pick a solo that I love, over a specific tune that I want to learn." "Hate Coltrane and never listen to him? Then don't transcribe him." Start with the legends, not modern players. — [JazzAdvice, What, Why...](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/what-why-where-who-when-and-how-to-transcribe/). "If you are excited about the solo you are learning, it can make a world of difference." — [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/how-to-learn/)
- **Process over choice:** "it's not so much the solo that you choose that's important, rather the process that you go through in order to learn it." — [JazzAdvice, 3 Secrets](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/the-3-secrets-to-transcribing-jazz-solos/)
- **Same instrument vs. others:** Pianist Terry Seabrook recommends relatively uncomplicated players on your own instrument (John Lewis, Horace Silver for piano) and a few favorite bars or one 12-bar chorus. — [Sussex Jazz Mag, Seabrook Tip No. 13](https://www.sussexjazzmag.com/improv-column-terry-seabrooks-jazz-tip-of-the-month-no-13/). (JazzAdvice's free "Getting Started" page gives no instrument recommendation.) On the other side, a jazzguitar.be poster says horn solos improve phrasing and help you avoid instrument clichés. — [jazzguitar.be](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/77772-any-advice-transcribing-other-instruments-besides-guitar.html)
- PianoGroove also suggests starting with a short line over a ii-V-I rather than a full solo. — [PianoGroove (search snippet)](https://www.pianogroove.com/?p=584)

### Inferences
- For this learner, a reasonable first target is one chorus (12 bars) of a medium-tempo blues solo from the shortlist in Q1, or a single 4-bar phrase if even that feels big. The whole solo can come later.
- Seabrook's "own instrument first" advice conflicts somewhat with this project's choice of vocal, guitar, and vibes sources. The conflict is small: single-line guitar and vibes lines map onto a pianist's right hand more directly than dense two-handed piano solos do, and singing-first works best with monophonic lines.

### Gaps
- No source gives a typical duration (hours, days, weeks) for a first solo. Learn Jazz Standards only says repetition may run into "hundreds of time[s]" and "Sound like a lot of work? It is."

## 4. Method: steps, and the writing-it-down debate

### Takeaway
The consensus sequence: listen repeatedly, sing along (slowed down if needed), sing without the recording, find it on the instrument, play along with the recording for exact feel and articulation, analyze it against the chords, extract and transpose lines (12 keys), then apply them to other tunes. Writing it down is optional and should come after the line is learned by ear.

### Cited Findings
- **JazzAdvice sequence:** listen and loop; slow to 50% in Transcribe!; "sing the line along with the recording, dialing in every note perfectly"; learn it as one continuous phrase; raise the speed while you can still sing it; go back to half speed and play along, then speed up; analyze (starting chord tone, direction, concepts); play in all 12 keys and apply it to tunes. — [JazzAdvice, Getting Started](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/getting-started-with-transcription/)
- **Learn Jazz Standards sequence:** pick a solo you enjoy, learn to sing it, learn one short phrase at a time, repeat each phrase with the recording ("Playing along with the recording holds you accountable to playing exactly what the soloist played"), chain phrases into sections (e.g., 32 bars), then review. "Jazz is an aural language," so reading a transcription is "never the best way to learn a jazz solo." — [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/how-to-learn/)
- **Beyond the notes:** memorize in all 12 keys, identify the chords, analyze why the lines work, apply them, and build exercises from them. Transcribe for "style, time, phrasing, and articulation" too. — [JazzAdvice, 3 Secrets](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/the-3-secrets-to-transcribing-jazz-solos/)
- **Sing with, then without, the recording:** A cafesaxophone forum poster recommends singing along, then singing without the recording, and only then moving to the instrument, calling it the fastest route to memory. Anecdotal. — [Cafe Saxophone](https://cafesaxophone.com/threads/what-how-and-why-are-you-transcribing.13137/page-4)
- **Seabrook:** learn to sing some or all of it, learn the articulation and groove that paper misses, try it in another key, and memorize and analyze its harmonic, rhythmic, and melodic features. — [Sussex Jazz Mag](https://www.sussexjazzmag.com/improv-column-terry-seabrooks-jazz-tip-of-the-month-no-13/)
- **Writing it down, against or deferred:** "Many great players never wrote anything down, others wrote down everything." "Wait until you're done transcribing your solo, chorus, or line with your instrument before you write anything down." "Do not use what you write down as a crutch." Transcription books are "a watered down approximation." — [JazzAdvice, What, Why...](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/what-why-where-who-when-and-how-to-transcribe/)
- **Writing it down, in favor:** PianoGroove (Hayden Hill) recommends a book of blank staff paper and a pencil, then MuseScore once confident. A commenter adds, "You're not obliged to notate the notes if you don't want to." — [PianoGroove, Introduction to Transcription](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/). Learn Jazz Standards says writing builds notation and analysis skills but is a complement, not a replacement. — [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/how-to-learn/)
- **Feel over precision:** a forum poster concluded "it's more important to feel what is going on than to try to nail that dotted 1/128th note." — [jazzguitar.be (search snippet)](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/106798-transcribing-vocalists.html)

### Inferences
- A classically trained pianist will be tempted to notate early, because reading is their strength. The educator consensus (JazzAdvice, LJS) suggests holding off on writing until the line is sung and played from memory. Writing can then serve as analysis, not as a crutch.

### Gaps
- No Hal Galper, Peter Martin/Open Studio, Jens Larsen, or Jamey Aebersold primary source on this topic was retrieved in this pass.

## 5. Tools: slow-down, looping, stem separation, pitch-shifting

### Takeaway
Transcribe! (desktop) and Amazing Slow Downer (iOS) are the tools educators mention most. Moises and Anytune add AI stem separation. Educators encourage slowing down ("No need to be macho") but treat it as scaffolding: the goal is to hear lines at full speed. I found no independent 2026 benchmark of stem separation on jazz.

### Cited Findings
- **Transcribe!:** recommended by JazzAdvice for looping and slowing. "No need to be macho." Start at 50%. — [JazzAdvice, What, Why...](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/what-why-where-who-when-and-how-to-transcribe/); [JazzAdvice, Getting Started](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/getting-started-with-transcription/). (JazzAdvice's Transcribe! links are affiliate links.) PianoGroove highlights its A/B loop, variable speed, and zoom. Its "Show Note Guesses" is "not always perfectly accurate, so you have to use your ears." — [PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/)
- **Amazing Slow Downer (iPhone/iPad, Spotify integration):** "very user friendly and intuitive to slow down, loop, and set start and end points." — [PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/). A Trumpet Herald user switched from ASD to Transcribe! and "never looked back." — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1732344)
- **SoundSlice:** shows the notes, which PianoGroove says "may be counterintuitive to the task at hand." — [PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/)
- **Anytune:** slows music without changing pitch, with looping and stem separation (from a Japanese-language guide, secondhand). — [MiniTool](https://moviemaker.minitool.com/jp/news/slow-down-music.html). **Audacity** is a free option. — [jazzguitar.be](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/83610-what-do-you-use-transcription.html)
- **Moises:** stem separation (vocals/drums/bass on free tier), chord detection, pitch shifting, BPM detection, click track. A competitor's review (StemSplit) says chord detection gets less reliable in harmonically complex genres such as jazz. Treat this with caution because of the source. Moises's self-reported claim is separation SDR more than 15% above its nearest competitor. — [StemSplit Moises review](https://stemsplit.io/blog/moises-ai-review); [Music.ai case study](https://music.ai/case-studies/education/moises-uses-music-ai-to-empower-millions/); [Sunowatermark Moises review 2026](https://sunowatermark.com/blog/moises-ai-review-2026/)
- **Pitfalls of slowing:** PianoGroove's stated goal is to "pick up material directly from recordings, without slowing it down," and to move from software note-guesses to relying solely on your ears. — [PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/). A jazzguitar.be thread argues for trying at full speed before slowing a segment. — [jazzguitar.be](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/improvisation/94714-transcribing-jazz-solos-tips-tricks-recommended-recordings.html). JazzAdvice: speed up gradually while you can still sing the line accurately. — [JazzAdvice, Getting Started](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/getting-started-with-transcription/)
- **Seabrook (older):** loop with CD cycle mode; slow-down software that keeps pitch; Transcribe! speed and cycle modes. — [Sussex Jazz Mag](https://www.sussexjazzmag.com/improv-column-terry-seabrooks-jazz-tip-of-the-month-no-13/)

### Inferences
- Stem separation is most useful for these targets on older, mono, or dense recordings: Holiday 1937 sides, Christian 1939, the guitar under Turrentine on *Midnight Blue*. Artifacts from separation could blur articulation and ghost notes, the very details being learned. Check the separated stem against the original mix.
- Pitch-shifting a vocal recording into the learner's comfortable singing range helps with the singing stage. Tradeoff (my inference): if the line is then learned on piano in the shifted key, it no longer matches the original record for play-along. Shifting by an octave keeps the key, and a pianist can simply play an octave up or down.

### Gaps
- No source covered YouTube's built-in playback-speed control for transcription.
- No independent 2026 comparison of AI stem-separation quality on jazz recordings was found. All quality claims are vendor or competitor marketing.
- No source directly compared pitch-shift quality across these apps.

## 6. Pianist-specific application of horn/vocal lines

### Takeaway
Sources say to keep the line in the right hand. Under it, play simple left-hand voicings (or just 3rds and 7ths) along with the recording to hear where line notes fall against the changes. Transpose to other keys after learning it. Focus on articulation and groove, accepting that piano can't reproduce bends, slides, and some ghost notes. Explicit guidance on original key vs. transposed during the learning stage was not found.

### Cited Findings
- **Left-hand voicings under the recording:** "play the left hand voicings underneath the recording" to see where melody notes fall against the changes. A chord on beat 1 of each bar is optional ("This is not an essential step"). If chords clash, "just play the 3rd and the 7th." — [PianoGroove, Introduction to Transcription](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/)
- **Start with the right-hand line:** one guide calls the melodic line usually the easiest part to hear. For monophonic (horn) solos, you listen for the chords implied by the line and the rhythm section. — [Ledutu (search snippet; low-authority aggregator)](https://ledutu.com/blogs/how-to-transcribe-jazz-piano-solos-effectively/)
- **Articulation and groove:** these don't translate to paper and must be learned from the record. — [Sussex Jazz Mag, Seabrook](https://www.sussexjazzmag.com/improv-column-terry-seabrooks-jazz-tip-of-the-month-no-13/). PianoGroove argues that absorbing phrasing and articulation is the main purpose of transcription, and the right notes are only half the job. — [PianoGroove (search snippet)](https://www.pianogroove.com/?p=584)
- **Instrument limits:** "Piano can't do all the stylistic slides, bends, slurs, and ghost notes," so the poster adapted those details. — [jazzguitar.be](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/77772-any-advice-transcribing-other-instruments-besides-guitar.html)
- **Keys:** learn transcribed lines in all 12 keys after mastering them. — [JazzAdvice, Getting Started](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/getting-started-with-transcription/); [JazzAdvice, 3 Secrets](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/the-3-secrets-to-transcribing-jazz-solos/). "Try some/all of it in another key." — [Seabrook](https://www.sussexjazzmag.com/improv-column-terry-seabrooks-jazz-tip-of-the-month-no-13/)
- **Play-along accountability:** play with the recording to match exactly what the soloist played, feel and phrasing included. — [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/how-to-learn/)

### Inferences
- A practical pianist sequence based on the sources above:
  1. Sing the line with the recording, then without it.
  2. Find it with the right hand alone, in the original key and register, so you can play along.
  3. Play along until the timing and accents match.
  4. Add left-hand 3rd/7th shells under the recording (PianoGroove).
  5. Play hands together without the recording.
  6. Analyze against the changes.
  7. Transpose and apply to other tunes.
- For horn and vocal articulation on piano, my inference (unsourced): ghost notes map to very soft, barely-sounded notes. Legato slurs and accented off-beat tonguing map to touch and dynamics. Bends and scoops map to grace-note slides from a half step below. A classical pianist's touch control is an advantage here. The main risk is evening out the rhythm into straight classical eighths instead of copying the soloist's swing and placement.
- Register matters: guitar sounds an octave lower than written, and vocals sit in the singer's range. Playing the line in the register heard on the record keeps the play-along stage accurate. This is my inference.
