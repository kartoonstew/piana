# First Tunes to Learn by Ear (Blues Heads, Riff Tunes, Simple Standards) for a Classically Trained Pianist New to Jazz

Research date: 2026-11-02. Scope: which heads to learn by ear first, which recordings to learn them from, in what order, and how.

**Note on sourcing for the writer.** Educator sources agree well on *which tunes* to start with and *how* to learn them. Most do NOT give a reference recording's key and tempo. Where this note gives a key or tempo that no fetched source supports, it is marked **[unverified, from general knowledge]** under Inferences. The learner should confirm it at the keyboard in the first minute of listening, which is itself a useful ear exercise. No melodies or transcriptions are reproduced here.

---

## Q1. Which blues heads and riff tunes do educators most recommend as first ear-learning targets?

### Takeaway
The educator lists overlap heavily on a core of six: C Jam Blues, Sonnymoon for Two, Bags' Groove, Mr. P.C., Now's the Time and Tenor Madness. BetterSax ranks them from easiest to hardest in roughly that order. Learn Jazz Standards (LJS) adds Blue Monk, Straight No Chaser, Things Ain't What They Used to Be, Blues in the Closet, Birk's Works, Freddie Freeloader, All Blues, Equinox, Footprints, Watermelon Man and Sandu as the next layer. C Jam Blues is the one tune every source calls the easiest.

### Cited Findings
- BetterSax's "6 easiest blues heads," in its own order of difficulty: **C Jam Blues** (Ellington, concert C; the melody uses only two notes, "an exercise in simplicity and minimalism"); **Sonnymoon for Two** (Bb; five notes, the minor pentatonic of the key; "the melody figure is played exactly the same way 3 times"); **Bags' Groove** (Milt Jackson, typically F; minor-pentatonic based, one phrase repeated three times); **Mr. P.C.** (Coltrane, minor blues; "our first melody that isn't just a straight up repeat," with the first phrase on i, the same phrase moved to iv, and a blues-scale answer); **Now's the Time** (Parker, F; the notes "aren't particularly difficult" but "the rhythm is quite complex and will be the biggest challenge"); **Tenor Madness** (Bb; the trickiest of the six but very repetitive, ending in "a very typical example of a bebop line") — [BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/the-6-easiest-blues-heads-you-must-know/)
- BetterSax recommends learning these by ear from the original recordings: "put on the original recordings and learn them by ear," so that they "stay with you for the rest of your life." It treats sheet music as a fallback. — [BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/the-6-easiest-blues-heads-you-must-know/)
- LJS's list of 28 blues heads labels these as easy or entry-level: C-Jam Blues ("easiest blues head of all time," only two notes), Bag's Groove ("easy blues head," concert F), Birk's Works ("easy minor blues head"), Blues in the Closet ("easy blues head"), Mr. P.C. ("entry level" minor blues), Things Ain't What They Used to Be ("entry level blues head"), Straight No Chaser ("simple" blues head) and Cool Blues ("simple blues head"). — [Learn Jazz Standards, 28 Jazz Blues Heads](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/28-jazz-blues-heads-you-need-to-know/)
- The same list's other tunes: All Blues (3/4), Au Privave and Billie's Bounce (bebop blues heads), Blue Monk (a "classic"), Blues for Alice and Chi Chi (Bird blues, alternate changes), Cheryl (concert C), Equinox (minor), Footprints (a variation of a minor blues), Freddie Freeloader (last chord varied), Freight Trane, Now's the Time, Relaxin' at Camarillo, Route 66, Sandu (Clifford Brown, concert Eb), Sonnymoon for Two, Take the Coltrane, Tenor Madness, Watermelon Man ("variation on a blues") and West Coast Blues (3/4). LJS advises picking three you don't know and learning them. — [Learn Jazz Standards, 28 Jazz Blues Heads](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/28-jazz-blues-heads-you-need-to-know/)
- LJS's "20 easy standards" list also includes blues-family tunes: All Blues, Blue Monk, Bags' Groove, C-Jam Blues, Cold Duck Time (Eddie Harris, a 12-bar straight-eighth funk blues, mostly I-IV), Mr. P.C., Watermelon Man and Work Song. — [Learn Jazz Standards, 20 Easy Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)
- A band-director clinician writing for DANSR says he uses Sonny Rollins's "Sonnymoon for Two" as his first head with students. — [DANSR, Learning to Play Jazz Heads During Rehearsals](https://www.dansr.com/resources/learning-to-play-jazz-heads-during-rehearsals) (seen only as a search snippet; I did not fetch the full page)
- JazzAdvice calls Tenor Madness a great beginner blues head to know. — [JazzAdvice, Tenor Madness](https://www.jazzadvice.com/jazz-standards/tenor-madness/) (search snippet)
- **Correction to a search summary.** One search summary said JazzAdvice called the "bridge" of Tenor Madness hard. A follow-up search found no such passage. Tenor Madness is a 12-bar blues in Bb with no bridge, and the "bridge" comments came from forum posts about "Milestones." Do not repeat that claim. — [search cross-check: JazzAdvice Tenor Madness page](https://www.jazzadvice.com/jazz-standards/tenor-madness/); [LJS Tenor Madness](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/jazz-originals/tenor-madness/)
- University of Utah's blues taxonomy: a "blues blues" uses only I7, IV7 and V7. A "jazz blues" adds ii-Vs (examples: Au Privave, Tenor Madness). A "Bird blues" is reharmonized (Chi-Chi, Blues for Alice). A minor blues example is Mr. PC. A modal blues example is Freddie Freeloader. Some blues put bVI7 or bV7 in bar 9 instead of V7 (Equinox, Cousin Mary). — [University of Utah Jazz Studies, Song List and Guidelines for Tune Memorization](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)

### Inferences
- Taken together, the sources give a strong consensus core of first blues heads: C Jam Blues, Sonnymoon for Two, Bags' Groove, Blue Monk, Now's the Time, Tenor Madness, Straight No Chaser, Things Ain't What They Used to Be, Mr. P.C., Freddie Freeloader and All Blues. Watermelon Man, Song for My Father and Cantaloupe Island are not 12-bar blues, but they are "blues-adjacent" 16-bar riff tunes and fit naturally after the blues.
- Billie's Bounce, Au Privave, Blues for Alice and Chi Chi are standard blues heads, but none of the sources call them easy. For a pure ear learner they are a second-tier goal, after the riff blues.
- Cantaloupe Island did not appear in any fetched educator list. I include it on general-knowledge grounds: its pentatonic riff melody and three-chord 16-bar form make it ear-friendly. That inclusion is mine, not sourced.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch Open Studio/Peter Martin, Jazz Piano School, PianoGroove, Piano With Jonny, Ethan Iverson, Hal Galper or Jens Larsen material on first tunes in the time budget. Their tune lists are not represented here.
- The Reddit search returned no actual r/JazzPiano or r/Jazz threads on "first tunes by ear," only sidebar text (see Q6).

## Q2. Which simple standards are recommended for ear learning, and which are flagged as harder than they look?

### Takeaway
The most commonly recommended first non-blues standards are Autumn Leaves, Blue Bossa, Summertime, St. Thomas, Doxy, Satin Doll, Take the A Train, Song for My Father, Watermelon Man, So What, Lester Leaps In and Tune Up. Sources flag specific "harder than it looks" points rather than whole tunes:
- **Blue Monk:** its rhythm.
- **Now's the Time:** its rhythm.
- **Satin Doll:** bars 5–6.
- **Autumn Leaves:** the chromatic descent near the end.
- **Blue Bossa:** the key change.
- **Mr. P.C.:** its tempo.

### Cited Findings
- LJS's 20 easy standards, with the recordings it names:
  - All Blues: Miles Davis, *Kind of Blue* (1959)
  - Autumn Leaves: Cannonball Adderley, *Somethin' Else* (1958); 32-bar AABC
  - Blue Monk: Thelonious Monk Trio (1954)
  - Blue Bossa: "Kenny Dorham, *Page One*" (1963); 16 bars
  - Bags' Groove: Milt Jackson, *Wizard of the Vibes* (1952), and Miles Davis, *Bags' Groove* (1957)
  - C-Jam Blues: Ellington (1942)
  - Cold Duck Time: Eddie Harris, *Swiss Movement* (1969)
  - Doxy: Sonny Rollins, *Miles Davis with Sonny Rollins* (1954); key Bb; all dominant chords; a contrafact of "Ja-Da"
  - Fly Me to the Moon: key C; 32-bar AABA
  - Mack the Knife: key Bb; four chords
  - Lester Leaps In: Lester Young (1939); simplified rhythm changes
  - Mr. P.C.: Coltrane, *Giant Steps* (1960); minor blues
  - My Little Suede Shoes: Parker, *Swedish Schnapps* (1951); key Eb
  - Song for My Father: Horace Silver (1964); key F minor
  - St. Thomas: Rollins, *Saxophone Colossus* (1956); 16 bars; "fairly repetitive" harmony
  - Summertime: key A minor; 16 bars
  - There Is No Greater Love
  - Watermelon Man: Herbie Hancock, *Takin' Off* (1962); 16-bar straight-eighth
  - Work Song: Nat Adderley (1960); key F minor; 16 bars
  - What Is This Thing Called Love

  — [Learn Jazz Standards, 20 Easy Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)
- Keyboardimprov's "Tier 1: tunes to begin with," which it calls "jam session basics": All Of Me, Autumn Leaves, Black Orpheus, Blue Bossa, Blue Monk, Four, Footprints, How High The Moon, Misty, Mr. P.C., So What, Song For My Father, Take The 'A' Train, Wave, Tune Up. **Tier 2** ("slightly more challenging") includes All Blues, Girl From Ipanema, My Funny Valentine, Satin Doll, Stella By Starlight, 'Round Midnight and There Is No Greater Love. **Tier 3** ("intermediate") includes Solar, Oleo, Confirmation, Donna Lee and Giant Steps. — [Keyboardimprov, Real Book tunes you should learn first](https://keyboardimprov.com/here-are-the-real-book-tunes-you-should-learn-first/)
- Free Jazz Lessons' six easy standards are Satin Doll, Autumn Leaves, Blue Bossa, Girl From Ipanema, Green Dolphin Street and Fly Me to the Moon. It notes that much of learning jazz tunes is "based off listening to 'reference' recordings." — [Free Jazz Lessons](https://freejazzlessons.com/?p=667) (search snippet)
- A jazzguitar.be forum thread names Satin Doll, Autumn Leaves and How High the Moon as common first tunes. — [jazzguitar.be forum](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/songs/4557-best-first-jazz-standard-learn.html) (search snippet; forum opinion)
- **Harder-than-it-looks flags:**
  - **Blue Monk:** simple melody, but its rhythm is "syncopated and complex." — [LJS 20 Easy](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)
  - **Now's the Time:** "the rhythm is quite complex and will be the biggest challenge." — [BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/the-6-easiest-blues-heads-you-must-know/)
  - **Blue Bossa:** watch the "chromatic key change." — [LJS 20 Easy](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)
  - **Mr. P.C.:** the original is "fairly fast," so slow it down for practice. — [LJS 20 Easy](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)
  - **Fly Me to the Moon:** has "a few non-diatonic surprises." — [LJS 20 Easy](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)
  - **Satin Doll:** the A section is riff-like and moves almost entirely by step, but the chords in bars 5–6 "almost defy analysis." Key C major, AABA; the bridge moves through ii-V-I in F and G. — [JazzStandards.com, Satin Doll](https://www.jazzstandards.com/compositions-0/satindoll.htm)
  - **Autumn Leaves:** JazzStandards.com calls it one of the first tunes novices learn. Its main challenge is six bars from the end, where the chords descend chromatically through tritone substitutions. — [JazzStandards.com, Autumn Leaves](https://www.jazzstandards.com/compositions-0/autumnleaves.htm)
- LJS defines "intermediate" as more than one key center or scale, more than 4–6 chords, harder to improvise over, and perhaps a more complicated melody. It says most standards are probably intermediate. — [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=24053) (search snippet)

### Inferences
- Misty, My Funny Valentine, Solar and Stella By Starlight appear on some beginner lists (Misty is in keyboardimprov's Tier 1). By the LJS "intermediate" criteria, though, they are harder to learn **by ear** than they look. Misty and My Funny Valentine are ballads, and recordings take them rubato with heavy melodic embellishment. Solar has moving key centers in a short, compressed form. I suggest deferring all four until after the blues and the 16-bar tunes. This is my inference; only keyboardimprov placed them by tier.
- Bye Bye Blackbird and There Will Never Be Another You did not appear in any fetched beginner list. They are reasonable early-songbook choices (singable, with lyrics and well-known vocal versions), but that judgment is unsourced.

### Gaps
- I found no single authoritative source listing "standards beginners mistakenly think are easy." The flags above are tune-by-tune remarks gathered from several sources.

## Q3. What criteria do educators give for choosing a tune to learn by ear?

### Takeaway
Sources converge on these criteria:
- a short form (12 or 16 bars)
- few chords, or a single tonality
- a melody built on repetition or riffs, or on a small set of notes such as two notes or the minor pentatonic
- a melody you can sing
- a famous, clear recording, preferably a single horn or a vocalist stating the melody plainly
- a tune you actually like

### Cited Findings
- LJS criteria for "easy" tunes: harmonically, rhythmically and melodically simple; often short (12 or 16 bars); few chords or staying in one tonality; simple melodies or repetitive harmony that are easy to memorize; built on important progressions. — [LJS 20 Easy](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)
- BetterSax ranks heads by how few notes the melody uses (two notes, then five), how literally the phrase repeats (three identical statements), and whether pitch or rhythm is the hard part. — [BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/the-6-easiest-blues-heads-you-must-know/)
- Utah says to start from "a high-quality recording of the song by a famous jazz performer." For Great American Songbook tunes it recommends learning from singers such as Ella Fitzgerald or Frank Sinatra, "because vocalists often sing relatively straightforward versions of the head," and the lyrics help you remember the notes and rhythm. — [University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)
- Search snippets from educator sites recommend Sinatra for an accurate melody, but warn that Billie Holiday "takes lots of liberties" with melodies and that bebop tunes often lack lyrics. — [LJS, How do I know the real melody to a jazz standard](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/ljs-podcast/how-do-i-know-the-real-melody-to-a-jazz-standard/) (search snippet)
- LJS: choose tunes you like, because tunes learned out of obligation don't stick. — [LJS, How to learn a jazz standard and not forget it](https://learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard-and-not-forget-it)
- Keyboardimprov: learn tunes your group plays or that you've heard on a recording that interests you; go from basic to advanced; work on one song at a time. — [Keyboardimprov](https://keyboardimprov.com/here-are-the-real-book-tunes-you-should-learn-first/)
- A piano teacher's blog says they recommend Oscar Peterson's *Night Train* to students as a first jazz album, partly because its relatively simple harmony makes it easy to play along with. — [search summary of jazz blog commentary on *Night Train*](https://musicophilesblog.com/2016/02/02/oscar-petersons-night-train/) (search snippet; attribution to a specific page is not fully certain)

### Inferences
- For a classically trained pianist, a piano-led reference recording adds something horn recordings lack: the left hand and comping are audible in the same instrument the learner plays. Oscar Peterson Trio, *Night Train* (Verve, rec. Dec 16 1962, released 1963) contains C-Jam Blues, Bag's Groove, Things Ain't What They Used to Be and Band Call — [JazzFuel, Night Train](https://jazzfuel.com/oscar-peterson-trio-night-train/). That makes it an efficient second reference for several blues heads. Use it alongside the horn originals, though, not instead of them. Peterson's heads are embellished, and JazzFuel notes he plays Bags' Groove in **G**, not the usual F.

## Q4. What learning order do educators suggest?

### Takeaway
No source prescribes the exact sequence "riff blues → bebop blues → minor blues → modal → ii-V standards → rhythm changes." The ordering inside the BetterSax and keyboardimprov lists, though, implies a progression very close to it:
1. riff blues heads, where the same phrase repeats
2. a minor blues whose riff is moved to the iv chord
3. rhythmically complex bebop blues heads
4. 16-bar Latin, modal and funky tunes
5. ii-V standards
6. rhythm changes, with Lester Leaps In as the easy entry point

### Cited Findings
- BetterSax's sequence runs: two notes (C Jam), then a repeated pentatonic riff (Sonnymoon, Bags'), then a minor blues with the riff moved to iv (Mr. P.C.), then bebop rhythm (Now's the Time), then a bebop line (Tenor Madness). — [BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/the-6-easiest-blues-heads-you-must-know/)
- Keyboardimprov's tiers go from Tier 1 (blues, modal, simple standards) to Tier 2 (bossas, ballads, Satin Doll) to Tier 3 (bebop and harder harmony: Confirmation, Donna Lee, Solar, Oleo, Giant Steps). It says to go "from basic to advanced." — [Keyboardimprov](https://keyboardimprov.com/here-are-the-real-book-tunes-you-should-learn-first/)
- LJS presents Lester Leaps In as a simplified rhythm-changes tune with a simple melody, useful for AABA training. — [LJS 20 Easy](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)
- Utah's taxonomy implicitly runs from "blues blues" (I-IV-V) to jazz blues (ii-Vs) to Bird blues to minor and modal blues. — [University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)

## Q5. Which specific recordings are recommended as the reference version for each tune, and why?

### Takeaway
Educators mostly name the original or most famous recording, usually a single horn stating the head, as the reference. Utah adds a vocal version for songbook tunes. Piano-led alternatives with explicit sources are Monk Trio (Blue Monk), Peterson *Night Train* (C Jam, Bags', Things Ain't) and Bill Evans *Portrait in Jazz* (Autumn Leaves).

### Cited Findings
- Recording citations from LJS and BetterSax are collected in the Q2 and Q4 tables.
- BetterSax says to learn from "the original recordings." It singles out *Tenor Madness* as "the only recording where we have the masters" Rollins and Coltrane together. — [BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/the-6-easiest-blues-heads-you-must-know/)
- JazzStandards.com's Autumn Leaves picks: Cannonball Adderley, *Somethin' Else* (1958), a recording that "has inspired generations of jazz players"; Bill Evans, *Portrait in Jazz* (1959); Ahmad Jamal (1955); Erroll Garner, *Concert by the Sea* (1955); Wynton Marsalis, *Standard Time Vol. 1* (1986); Bobby Timmons, *Live at the Village Vanguard* (1961). — [JazzStandards.com](https://www.jazzstandards.com/compositions-0/autumnleaves.htm)
- JazzStandards.com's Satin Doll picks: Duke Ellington (Ken Burns *JAZZ* collection); Earl Hines, *Live at the New School* (1973, solo piano); Ella Fitzgerald with Count Basie (1963); and trio versions including McCoy Tyner, *Nights of Ballads and Blues* (1963), and Jimmy Smith, *Organ Grinder Swing* (1965). — [JazzStandards.com](https://www.jazzstandards.com/compositions-0/satindoll.htm)
- Utah: find "a high-quality recording of the song by a famous jazz performer," and for songbook tunes use singers (Ella, Sinatra) for straightforward heads and lyrics. — [University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)
- Oscar Peterson Trio, *Night Train*: Verve, recorded Dec 16 1962, released 1963, with Ray Brown and Ed Thigpen. Tracks include C-Jam Blues, Bag's Groove (played in G), Things Ain't What They Used to Be and Band Call. — [JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/oscar-peterson-trio-night-train/)

### Inferences
- For this learner, a sound default is to learn the head from the horn original, then listen to a piano version to hear left-hand comping. The horn gives the clearest single-line melody. The piano version shows how a pianist voices the same changes.
- *Kind of Blue* (Freddie Freeloader, All Blues, So What) is probably the best-known jazz album on streaming. Its clear modern stereo sound (recorded 1959) makes it unusually easy to hear the bass.
- Charlie Parker's 1945 Savoy masters and Lester Young's 1939 recording have lo-fi sound, which makes bass notes hard to hear. Pair them with a later, clearer cover for the harmony while keeping the original for phrasing. This is my inference; no source addresses it.

- I did not fetch jazzstandards.com pages for every tune, Ted Gioia's *The Jazz Standards*, or Jamey Aebersold's play-along keys.

## Q6. How do educators recommend learning a tune by ear?

### Takeaway
The sources agree on a consistent sequence:
1. Listen many times.
2. Sing the melody with the recording, then without it.
3. Find the melody on the instrument, phrase by phrase.
4. Find the bass roots.
5. Hear the chord qualities and the progressions between them.
6. Only then check a lead sheet, knowing that lead sheets are often wrong.
7. Keep playing the tune.

Use slow-down software such as Transcribe! for hard passages. Learn the lyrics for songbook tunes.

### Cited Findings
- **JazzAdvice's sequence:**
  - Melody first: "Listen to the melody on repeat until you can confidently sing it along with the recording note for note," then find it on the piano.
  - Bass line second: listen selectively for roots on beats 1 and 3, and play them in the left hand.
  - Chord qualities third: major, minor, dominant or half-diminished.
  - Then the progressions between chords, such as ii-V-I.
  - Analysis last: chord tones, form and key centers.
  - When notes are hard to hear, slow the recording down. It names Transcribe! as "an invaluable tool."
  - Its principle: "if you can't hear it, you can't learn it."

  — [JazzAdvice, How to Learn Jazz Standards with the Piano](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-jazz-standards-with-the-piano/)
- **University of Utah's steps:** no lead sheet "until step 4 or later":
  1. Find a high-quality recording by a famous performer; use vocal versions for songbook tunes.
  2. "Listen to the recording with focused attention 5–10 times in a row," noting the changes, form and phrase structure ("Is it a 12-bar blues, or a 32-bar AABA song?").
  3. "Sing along with the melody several times," matching contour and rhythm.
  4. Play the melody with the recording by ear, phrase by phrase or note by note. Use pause and rewind ("you can also use an app or a digital audio workstation to slow down the recording or loop parts of it"). If 1–3 attempts miss, move by half steps methodically.
  5. Only then check a lead sheet, which may be inaccurate.

  — [University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)
- **Utah's definition of knowing a tune:** you can play the melody from memory in time, play the changes, notate the tune as a lead sheet, talk through it in roman numerals, and transpose it to a few keys on the spot. — [University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)
- **LJS:**
  - "listen to it many, many times" before working anything out.
  - Learn from several recordings, not a fakebook, because "Just because it's on a piece of paper doesn't mean it's correct."
  - "If you can sing it, you can play it."
  - Learn both melody and harmony.
  - Keep calling the tune at jams.

  — [LJS, How to learn a jazz standard and not forget it](https://learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard-and-not-forget-it)
- LJS's "LIST" method stands for Listen, Internalize, Sing, Transfer. Sing the melody with and without the recording before touching the instrument. — [LJS, Learn Jazz Standards the Smart Way (PDF)](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf) (search snippet)
- LJS argues that learning lyrics helps internalize melodies. Dexter Gordon's saying that knowing the lyrics helps you communicate a song is quoted secondhand on a blog. I could not verify the original source. — [LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=37824); [jazzleadsheets.com, Dexter Gordon](https://jazzleadsheets.com/news/tag/dexter-gordon/) (search snippets; the quote's attribution is unverified)
- The r/JazzPiano sidebar guidance includes "Learn the melody by ear" and "Learn the chord changes to your favorite songs by ear." It suggests *Jazz Piano for the Classical Pianist* (Justin Highland) for classical crossover players. — [r/JazzPiano mirror](https://lr.in.psf.lt/r/Jazzpiano) (search snippet via a Reddit mirror; not verified directly on reddit.com)
- Pianote says master jazz pianists suggest putting lead sheets aside as soon as you can, and memorizing and learning tunes by ear. — [Pianote, Easy Jazz Standards](https://www.pianote.com/blog/easy-jazz-standards/) (search snippet)

### Inferences
- For a classically trained pianist, the main change is reversing the usual order: sing and hear before reading. Utah's "no lead sheet until step 4" rule is the clearest guardrail.
- Slow-down tools mentioned or implied across sources: Transcribe! (JazzAdvice), "an app or a DAW" (Utah), and YouTube's playback-speed control. Amazing Slow Downer was not mentioned by any fetched source. It is a well-known equivalent, but that is unsourced.
- Blues heads have no lyrics, so the "learn the lyrics" advice applies from Summertime and Satin Doll onward. Satin Doll has lyrics by Johnny Mercer [unverified]. For blues heads, singing the riff and the bass roots does the same job.

### Gaps
- Open Studio (Peter Martin), Hal Galper, Barry Harris-lineage and Jens Larsen descriptions of ear-learning method were not retrieved.
- No source gave evidence comparing methods (for example, whether singing first improves retention). The advice is expert opinion only.
