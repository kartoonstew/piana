# Digestible Trumpet, Cornet, and Trombone Solos for First Learn-by-Ear Targets

Research pass: 2026-11-02. Learner context: advanced classical pianist, new to jazz, singing horn lines first and then playing them on piano. No transcriptions or notation reproduced; recordings only.

Source-access notes, for the report writer:
- jazztrumpetsolos.com now returns a 301 redirect to an unrelated site (youngadultreads.com), so it could not be used for timings. allsolos.com was used instead for start times, chorus counts, tempos, and keys.
- Trumpet Herald forum pages returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch. Claims attributed to Trumpet Herald below come from search-result snippets only, not full-page reads, so treat them as lower confidence.
- No Reddit threads (r/jazz, r/trumpet, r/jazzpiano) appeared in searches. No Open Studio, Jens Larsen, or Jason Lyon beginner brass list was found.

## Which brass solos do educators most often name as great first transcriptions, ordered easiest to harder?

### Takeaway
Miles Davis on "Freddie Freeloader" and "So What" (Kind of Blue, 1959) are the most consistently named brass first transcriptions in the sources found. A second tier, each named by one or two sources, is Chet Baker's vocal-album solos ("There Will Never Be Another You", "But Not for Me"), Harry "Sweets" Edison ("Sermonette", "Pennies from Heaven"), Miles's slow "Trane's Blues", Kenny Dorham (Blue Bossa, Chicago Blues, Quiet Kenny), and Armstrong's "Struttin' with Some Barbecue". The source search turned up no specific trombone solo recommended as a first transcription.

### Cited Findings

Per-solo data (most verified details come from allsolos.com):

1. **Harry "Sweets" Edison, "Sermonette" (Lambert, Hendricks & Ross recording)**: In a Society for Music Theory discussion thread, a contributor (Joon Park) describes it as "two very simple choruses with lots of feels", each chorus about one minute long. The instructor who asked (Zac Cairns) judged it the best fit for his students that semester. — [SMT Discuss thread](https://discuss.societymusictheory.org/discussion/comment/1422)
2. **Miles Davis, "So What", *Kind of Blue* (1959)**: The solo starts at 1:30 and ends at 3:26. It is 2 choruses of a 32-bar AABA form at 136 bpm, medium swing, in concert D minor (E minor for trumpet). Miles takes the first solo and Coltrane follows. — [allsolos.com](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/90/miles-davis-trumpet-solo-on-so-what)
   - Learn Jazz Standards: "simple but beautifully melodic", "easy to learn", and "a great example of modal playing". — [Learn Jazz Standards, 5 Jazz Solos You Need To Learn](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/5-jazz-solos-you-need-to-learn/)
   - JazzFuel: "substantial use of space, hip phrasing and understated mid-register melodicism". — [JazzFuel, 11 of the Best Jazz Solos](https://jazzfuel.com/?p=52678)
   - A Trumpet Herald poster suggests learning the first chorus of the So What solo by ear as a way to start (search snippet only). — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=183092)
3. **Miles Davis, "Freddie Freeloader", *Kind of Blue* (1959)**: The solo runs 2:12 to 4:30. It is 6 choruses of a 12-bar blues at 127 bpm in concert B-flat (C for trumpet). Miles is the second soloist, after Wynton Kelly and before Coltrane. allsolos describes it as built "from short melodic fragments". — [allsolos.com](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/94/miles-davis-trumpet-solo-on-freddie-freeloader)
   - Learn Jazz Standards lists it first of its five solos and calls it "simple and not hard to lift", "not too difficult to learn", and "packed full of incredible melodic lines and beautiful phrasing". It also recommends learning it by ear before checking a transcription. — [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/5-jazz-solos-you-need-to-learn/)
   - A Trumpet Herald thread recommends it to beginning transcribers. One poster says it was the only complete solo they transcribed in their first year and a half of serious study (search snippet only). — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=954054)
4. **Chet Baker, "There Will Never Be Another You" (mid-1950s vocal record)**: JazzFuel calls it "a short, extremely singable mid-register trumpet solo". It teaches using the melody as a starting point and covers major and minor ii-V-Is. JazzFuel does not name the album. — [JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/?p=52678)
5. **Chet Baker, "But Not for Me", *Chet Baker Sings* (1954)**:
   - Recommended in the SMT thread, with the trumpet solo at roughly 1:22 in the video that thread linked. Timestamps may differ across releases. — [SMT Discuss thread](https://discuss.societymusictheory.org/discussion/comment/1422)
   - A Universidad San Francisco de Quito thesis transcribes and analyzes this solo, framed around why it is simple. Its keywords point to stepwise motion and repeated patterns. Only the abstract and keywords were seen, not the full text. — [USFQ thesis](https://repositorio.usfq.edu.ec/bitstream/23000/10918/1/128103.pdf)
6. **Miles Davis, "Trane's Blues"**: JazzAdvice says "It's slow and easy to approach", with phrases so defined that "it's as if Miles composed the solo before-hand". JazzAdvice does not name the album. — [JazzAdvice, 8 Awesome Blues Solos for Beginners to Transcribe](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/8-awesome-blues-solos-for-beginners-to-transcribe/)
7. **Miles Davis, "All Blues", *Kind of Blue* (1959)**: The solo runs 1:45 to 3:53. It is 4 choruses of a 12-bar blues at 138 bpm in concert G (A for trumpet), with Miles soloing first. The page is internally inconsistent on meter, calling it both 6/8 and a "6/4 blues". — [allsolos.com](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/102/miles-davis-trumpet-solo-on-all-blues)
8. **Kenny Dorham, "Blue Bossa", on Joe Henderson's *Page One* (1963)**: The solo runs 1:01 to 2:36. It is 4 choruses of a 16-bar form at 162 bpm, bossa nova feel, in concert C minor (D minor for trumpet). Dorham, who composed the tune, solos first, in a "warm, lyrical", "understated" style. — [allsolos.com](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/299/kenny-dorham-trumpet-solo-on-blue-bossa)
9. **Miles Davis, "Doxy" and "Four" (album *Blue Haze* named for "Four")**: Trumpet Herald posters suggest both as simple, clear examples, and one educator calls "Four" from the *Blue Haze* CD easy to transcribe (search snippet only). — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=183092)
10. **Chet Baker, general**: A Trumpet Herald moderator says his first transcriptions were Chet Baker solos because he "could usually keep up and figure out what he was playing". Other posters name "Tangerine" and "Well You Needn't" (search snippet only). — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=183092)
11. **Harry "Sweets" Edison, "Pennies from Heaven", *Pres & Sweets* (1955, with Lester Young)**: The solo runs 1:34 to 3:01. It is 2 choruses of a 32-bar ABAC form at 180 bpm in concert B-flat (C for trumpet). allsolos describes "spare, blues-inflected" playing that moves from short, rhythmically sharp phrases to more elaborate ones. — [allsolos.com](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/2373/harry-sweets-edison-trumpet-solo-on-pennies-from-heaven)
    - A Trumpet Herald poster says one of their first memorized solos was by Harry Edison and advises beginners to pick solos that "aren't particularly long" (search snippet only). — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1716909)
12. **Louis Armstrong, "Struttin' with Some Barbecue", Hot Five (1927)**: JazzFuel recommends it for "pre-bebop language" and "strong quarter note-based rhythms", noting that Armstrong plays "over offbeat stop time". — [JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/?p=52678)
13. **Kenny Dorham, "Chicago Blues"**: JazzAdvice says "the eighth note lines are fairly simple rhythmically and harmonically", his bebop language is "easy to conceptualize", and Dorham also sings on the recording. JazzAdvice does not name the album. — [JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/8-awesome-blues-solos-for-beginners-to-transcribe/)
14. **Kenny Dorham, *Quiet Kenny* (album, no single track named)**: A Trumpet Herald poster signed "jason" recommends starting with this album. The same poster calls Freddie Hubbard "tougher" but suggests "Watermelon Man" from Herbie Hancock's *Takin' Off* (search snippet only, and the poster's identity is unconfirmed). — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1716909)
15. **Freddie Hubbard, "Blues for Brenda"**: This appears on a class transcription assignment list (a Scribd document, seen only as a snippet) alongside Art Farmer material. — [Scribd "Transcribing Class"](https://www.scribd.com/doc/262920452/Transcribing-Class)

Trombone:
- Earl MacDonald's transcription page lists J.J. Johnson, Frank Rosolino, and Curtis Fuller as trombonists to consider, and Blue Mitchell, Kenny Dorham, Lee Morgan, Clifford Brown, Miles Davis, and Woody Shaw on trumpet. It names no specific solos. The page's one specific student recommendation is Ella Fitzgerald's vocal on "Smooth Sailing", for its "nice manageable tempo". — [Earl MacDonald](https://www.earlmacdonald.com/jazz-solo-transcription/)

Non-brass, but recurring in the same lists:
- Nat King Cole, "Love": a Trumpet Herald educator starts students on it because it is achievable "in one sitting" (search snippet only). — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1716909)
- Sarah Vaughan, "All of Me" (*Swinging Easy*): suggested in the SMT thread. — [SMT Discuss thread](https://discuss.societymusictheory.org/discussion/comment/1422)

### Inferences
- **Proposed easiest-to-harder order.** This is my synthesis. Sources do not rank these against each other.
  1. Sweets Edison, "Sermonette": 2 short choruses, called "very simple".
  2. Miles, "So What": 2 choruses, lots of space, a static modal background. It is easy to sing, and only two chords need tracking.
  3. Miles, "Freddie Freeloader": a blues at 127 bpm, built from short fragments. It is 6 choruses long, so learn 1 or 2 choruses first.
  4. Chet Baker, "There Will Never Be Another You": short, singable, mid-register, close to the melody.
  5. Chet Baker, "But Not for Me": stepwise and repetitive, on a standard with ii-Vs.
  6. Miles, "Trane's Blues": slow blues with composed-sounding phrases.
  7. Miles, "All Blues": 4 choruses over a waltz-like blues feel. The meter feel may confuse a newcomer.
  8. Kenny Dorham, "Blue Bossa": 4 choruses of a short 16-bar minor form at 162 bpm with straight-eighth bossa feel. It teaches minor ii-V-i.
  9. Miles, "Doxy" and "Four": medium swing, more chord changes.
  10. Kenny Dorham, "Chicago Blues" and *Quiet Kenny*: more eighth-note bebop lines.
  11. Sweets Edison, "Pennies from Heaven": the phrases are spare, but the tempo is 180 bpm.
  12. Armstrong, "Struttin' with Some Barbecue": stop-time, older vocabulary, and a cornet tone that may be harder to pitch-match on a 1927 recording.
  13. Freddie Hubbard, "Watermelon Man" and "Blues for Brenda": explicitly "tougher".
- For a classically trained pianist, *Kind of Blue* is an efficient first stop. Three digestible Miles solos (So What, Freddie Freeloader, All Blues) sit on one widely available album, and allsolos gives timings for all three.
- The Chet Baker picks matter for singing-first learning. Baker also sings the melody on *Chet Baker Sings*, so the learner hears the vocal melody and then a trumpet solo that stays close to it in the same register. That makes the link between melody and improvisation audible.
- Practical for the pianist: the allsolos concert keys (So What in D minor, Freddie Freeloader and Pennies from Heaven in B-flat, All Blues in G, Blue Bossa in C minor) are the keys to use on piano. The trumpet keys sound a whole step lower than written, so they should be ignored.

## Which solos are commonly flagged as deceptively hard?

### Takeaway
Few sources explicitly flag brass solos as hard. Freddie Hubbard is called "tougher" than Dorham. Parker's "Now's the Time" is "not the easiest" (alto, but often paired with Miles's solo on the same record). Coltrane's "Impressions" "isn't an easy one" (tenor). The only general warning found is that no solo is truly easy.

### Cited Findings
- JazzAdvice: "there's no such thing as an easy solo to transcribe". Its beginner picks are chosen because they are packed with information "in a fairly easy to hear way". — [JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/8-awesome-blues-solos-for-beginners-to-transcribe/)
- Freddie Hubbard is "tougher" than Kenny Dorham, per a Trumpet Herald poster (search snippet only). — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1716909)
- Learn Jazz Standards calls Parker's "Now's the Time" "not the easiest of solo's to learn". It rates Coltrane's "My Shining Hour" "a little bit more advanced" than Freddie Freeloader. — [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/5-jazz-solos-you-need-to-learn/)
- JazzFuel says Coltrane's "Impressions" "isn't an easy one to transcribe" (fast modal swing). JazzFuel also notes that on the 1945 Savoy "Now's the Time", Miles follows Parker with "a more diatonic solo". That makes Miles's solo the easier of the two on that record. — [JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/?p=52678)
- Per the SMT thread, Ella Fitzgerald's "How High the Moon" is "a bit faster" and its choruses "get progressively harder". — [SMT Discuss thread](https://discuss.societymusictheory.org/discussion/comment/1422)

### Inferences
- The likely deceptive cases are my own judgment, not sourced:
  - Freddie Freeloader is 6 choruses long, so length rather than note difficulty is the barrier.
  - "Pennies from Heaven" has spare phrases at a brisk 180 bpm.
  - "All Blues" has a 6/8 or 3/4 feel that can obscure where the downbeat is.
  - Armstrong's "West End Blues" opens with a famous virtuosic cadenza.
  - Lee Morgan's "The Sidewinder" and "Moanin'" are blues-based but rhythmically dense, with growls and half-valve effects.
  - Hubbard's "Red Clay" and "Blue Minor" are fast and harmonically dense.
- A sensible tactic is the JazzAdvice permission to learn only one chorus or a few phrases. That turns long solos like Freddie Freeloader into short targets.

### Gaps
- No fetched source explicitly flags West End Blues, The Sidewinder, Moanin', Red Clay, Blue Minor, or Clifford Brown as too hard for beginners. Those judgments above are inference only.
- No source discusses tone-related ear difficulties, such as Harmon-mute timbre or early-recording fidelity on Armstrong and Bix.

## What specific features make a horn solo digestible for a first transcription, according to educators?

### Takeaway
Educators converge on six features:
- medium or medium-slow 4/4 tempo
- short length, or permission to learn only part of a solo
- clearly defined, logically connected phrases separated by space
- standard and simple changes, especially the 12-bar blues
- a mix of rhythmic "statements" and simple eighth-note lines
- a mid-register, singable, melody-based approach

### Cited Findings
- JazzAdvice gives five criteria. — [JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/8-awesome-blues-solos-for-beginners-to-transcribe/)
  1. Basic 4/4 at a medium or medium-slow tempo.
  2. A mix of rhythmically decisive "statements" and flowing eighth-note lines.
  3. Clear phrasing that connects logically.
  4. Standard blues changes.
  5. Emotional impact: choose solos you love.
  - It also says blues are a good entry point because of their "simple and familiar chord changes", and that you don't need to transcribe a full chorus.
- Learn Jazz Standards praises the Freddie Freeloader solo for being "simple and not hard to lift". It also says the blues is "one of the most important forms in jazz to work on". — [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/5-jazz-solos-you-need-to-learn/)
- JazzFuel cites these digestibility features — [JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/?p=52678):
  - Chet Baker: "short, extremely singable mid-register", taking "the melody as a starting point".
  - Miles on So What: "substantial use of space", "understated mid-register melodicism".
  - Armstrong: "strong quarter note-based rhythms".
- Choose solos that "aren't particularly long" (Trumpet Herald, search snippet only). — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1716909)
- Chet Baker solos are approachable because the listener "could usually keep up and figure out what he was playing" (Trumpet Herald, search snippet only). — [Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=183092)
- Earl MacDonald's stated criterion is a "nice manageable tempo". — [Earl MacDonald](https://www.earlmacdonald.com/jazz-solo-transcription/)
- In the SMT thread, an educator describes a good assignment as "two very simple choruses", each about one minute. — [SMT Discuss thread](https://discuss.societymusictheory.org/discussion/comment/1422)
- The USFQ thesis on Chet Baker's "But Not for Me" highlights stepwise motion and repeated patterns (keywords only). A companion thesis on Baker's "It's Always You" notes that his phrases mostly run about two bars. — [USFQ thesis](https://repositorio.usfq.edu.ec/bitstream/23000/10918/1/128103.pdf)
- allsolos describes Miles on Freddie Freeloader as building from "short melodic fragments", and Sweets Edison on "Pennies from Heaven" as "spare" and "blues-inflected". — [allsolos Freddie Freeloader](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/94/miles-davis-trumpet-solo-on-freddie-freeloader); [allsolos Pennies from Heaven](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/2373/harry-sweets-edison-trumpet-solo-on-pennies-from-heaven)

### Inferences
- For a singing-first learner, the most important features are mid-register range, two-bar phrases with rests (breathing room to sing back), stepwise motion, and closeness to the tune's melody. Horn players' breath-length phrasing naturally produces these features, which supports the user's preference for horn over piano solos.
- What each solo is likely to teach, in my synthesis:
  - Blues phrasing: Freddie Freeloader, Trane's Blues, Sermonette, Chicago Blues.
  - Use of space and motivic fragments: So What, Freddie Freeloader.
  - Melodic paraphrase and guide tones over ii-Vs: Chet Baker's There Will Never Be Another You and But Not for Me.
  - Minor ii-V-i and Latin straight-eighth feel: Blue Bossa.
  - Swing-era rhythmic drive and quarter-note phrasing: Armstrong, Sweets Edison.
  - Bebop eighth-note lines: Dorham, Doxy, Four.

### Gaps
- No source gave a quantitative measure of digestibility, such as notes per bar or range in semitones.
- No source specifically addressed pianists transcribing horn solos, or singing before playing. That rationale is inference from general advice.

## Are there published or widely cited "first transcription" lists, and how do they overlap?

### Takeaway
The main citable lists are JazzAdvice's "8 Awesome Blues Solos for Beginners to Transcribe", Learn Jazz Standards' "5 Jazz Solos You Need To Learn", and JazzFuel's "11 of the Best Jazz Solos That Every Musician Should Transcribe" (not beginner-specific). Forum threads (Trumpet Herald, SMT Discuss) add more. Overlap among the brass picks centers on Miles Davis, especially *Kind of Blue*, with Chet Baker and Kenny Dorham as secondary overlaps.

### Cited Findings
- **JazzAdvice, 8 beginner blues solos** — [JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/8-awesome-blues-solos-for-beginners-to-transcribe/)
  - Brass: Miles Davis "Trane's Blues" and Kenny Dorham "Chicago Blues".
  - Others: Charlie Parker "Cool Blues", Sonny Rollins "Tenor Madness", Hank Mobley "Dig Dis" (*Soul Station*), Wynton Kelly "Dig Dis" (piano), Gene Ammons "Blues Up and Down" (*Boss Tenors*), Wes Montgomery "D-Natural Blues".
- **Learn Jazz Standards, 5 solos** — [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/5-jazz-solos-you-need-to-learn/)
  - Brass: Miles Davis "Freddie Freeloader" (listed first) and "So What".
  - Others: Coltrane "My Shining Hour", Parker "Now's the Time", Rollins "St. Thomas".
- **JazzFuel, 11 solos** — [JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/?p=52678)
  - Brass: Chet Baker "There Will Never Be Another You", Armstrong "Struttin' with Some Barbecue", Miles "So What".
  - Others: Lester Young, Parker, Getz, Coltrane, Dexter Gordon, Rollins, Bud Powell, Brecker.
- Learn Jazz Standards also hosts a 2014 PDF of "50 Easy Jazz Solo" recommendations from guitarist Jeff Libman, seen only via search snippet. Its contents were not retrieved. — [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=29055)
- **SMT Discuss thread, educators choosing an ear-transcription assignment**: Sweets Edison "Sermonette", Chet Baker "But Not for Me", Sarah Vaughan "All of Me", Ella Fitzgerald "How High the Moon", and Dave Marriott "Midnight Walk". — [SMT Discuss thread](https://discuss.societymusictheory.org/discussion/comment/1422)
- **Trumpet Herald threads** (search snippets only): Freddie Freeloader, So What (first chorus), Doxy, Four (*Blue Haze*), several Chet Baker solos (Tangerine, Well You Needn't), *Quiet Kenny*, a Sweets Edison solo, Hubbard "Watermelon Man" (flagged tougher), and Nat King Cole "Love". — [Trumpet Herald p=183092](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=183092); [p=954054](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=954054); [p=1716909](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1716909)
- **Earl MacDonald**: lists brass soloists to transcribe (Blue Mitchell, Dorham, Morgan, Clifford Brown, Miles, Woody Shaw; trombonists J.J. Johnson, Rosolino, Fuller) without naming solos. — [Earl MacDonald](https://www.earlmacdonald.com/jazz-solo-transcription/)

### Inferences
- Overlap tally for brass, counting the sources above (my count):
  - **So What:** 3 sources (Learn Jazz Standards, JazzFuel, Trumpet Herald).
  - **Freddie Freeloader:** 2 (Learn Jazz Standards, Trumpet Herald).
  - **Chet Baker (any solo):** 3 (JazzFuel, SMT thread, Trumpet Herald).
  - **Kenny Dorham (any solo):** 3 (JazzAdvice, Trumpet Herald, Earl MacDonald as a name only).
  - **Sweets Edison (any solo):** 2 (SMT thread, Trumpet Herald).
  - **Armstrong:** 1 (JazzFuel).
  - **Trane's Blues:** 1 (JazzAdvice).
- Miles on *Kind of Blue* is the closest thing to a consensus first brass transcription.
- Saxophone solos dominate the published lists. Brass-specific beginner lists are rare, and trombone lists were not found at all. The brass shortlist therefore draws partly on forum and educator-thread sources, which are lower confidence than the published articles.
