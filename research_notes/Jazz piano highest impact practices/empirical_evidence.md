# Empirical and Scholarly Evidence on Acquiring Jazz Improvisation Skill

Evidence-grade tags used below: [EXP] experimental/quasi-experimental; [CORR] correlational/regression; [CORPUS] computational analysis of recorded solos; [NEURO] brain imaging/EEG (usually small-N, cross-sectional); [QUAL] interview/ethnographic/case study; [OPINION] expert or practitioner opinion. Most findings below come from abstracts and secondary summaries; full texts were often paywalled (noted where relevant). Research current as of Nov 2026.

## 1. Aural/ear-based vs. notation-based learning (and effects on classically trained musicians)

### Takeaway
The most consistent quantitative finding is correlational: aural imitation ability (and accurate self-evaluation) predicts jazz improvisation achievement in college players. Classically trained musicians with no vernacular experience are measurably worse at playing by ear. Direct experimental comparisons of aural vs. notated instruction exist (Watson 2010), but I could not retrieve their results, so a causal claim that "aural beats notated" is not established by what I found.

### Cited Findings
- [CORR] May (2003), JRME 51(3): 245–258. N = 73 undergraduate wind players in college jazz ensembles at five Midwestern universities. Stepwise regression found **self-evaluation of improvisation** to be the strongest single predictor of improvisation achievement, with **aural imitation ability** second. Jazz theory knowledge and aural skills were also measured. Factor analysis yielded a single factor, suggesting instrumental jazz improvisation is one construct. Limitation: wind players only (no pianists), correlational. — [May 2003 record, Bibliojazz](https://bibliojazz-collegium-musicae.huma-num.fr/s/bibliojazz/item/13196); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/3345377); [Lissa May, Academia](https://indiana.academia.edu/LissaMay)
- [CORR] Madura (1996), JRME, vocal jazz improvisation: imitative ability was a significant predictor. For the composite score, predictors in order were jazz theory knowledge, imitative ability, then jazz experience. Imitative ability ranked first for the ii–V7–I task, and theory knowledge ranked first for the blues task. Voice lessons, gender, and general creativity did not predict achievement. — [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/3345598)
- [CORR] A University of Michigan thesis (2006–07) reported that aural imitation ability and technical facility are fundamental skills for improvisation achievement. — [Deep Blue, U. Michigan](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/e8a9d1ce-35f5-4dec-8a73-2b065ab78d7e)
- [CORR] An unattributed University of Miami dissertation-style model of jazz improvisation achievement (author and year not shown; possibly but not confirmed to be Ciorba) used self-assessment, self-efficacy, motivation, jazz theory knowledge, practice time, music aptitude, and listening experience as predictors. Combined, they accounted for about 50% of the variance. — [U. Miami scholarship repository](https://scholarship.miami.edu/permalink/01UOML_INST/105q9vf/alma991031447925502976)
- [CORR/quasi-EXP] Woody & Lehmann (2010), JRME 58(2): 101–115, "Student musicians' ear playing ability as a function of vernacular music experiences." Compared ear-playing in formally trained (classical-only) college musicians with musicians who also had vernacular (pop/jazz/folk) experience. Secondary sources report N ≈ 24. Vernacular musicians needed fewer attempts to reproduce melodies by ear, and singing back took fewer attempts than playing back on an instrument. Limitation: small N; figures from secondary citations, not full text. — [Woody CV, UNL](https://arts.unl.edu/faculty/cvs/Bob%20Woody%20June%202013%20CV.pdf); [Hildebrandt, PMEA summary](https://www.pmea.net/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/Hildebrandt.TeachingEarPlaying.PMEA_.2016.pdf); [UNL news on Woody](https://newsroom.unl.edu/announce/artsatunl/8953/53189)
- [EXP] Watson (2010), JRME 58(3), "The Effects of Aural Versus Notated Instructional Materials on Achievement and Self-Efficacy in Jazz Improvisation." Its stated purpose was to compare aural and notated pedagogical materials on improvisation achievement and self-efficacy. **I could not retrieve its results or N.** Sources also disagree on page range (240–259 vs. 219–239). — [Carleton jazz theory bibliography](https://jazztheory.ssac.carleton.ca/view.php?id=643); [SAGE TOC 58(3)](https://journals.sagepub.com/toc/jrm/58/3)
- [EXP] Davison (2006), UNT dissertation, aural vs. aural+notated modeling with grades 6–8 (N = 76). It reported significant gains in self-efficacy for improvising after improvisation instruction. The aural-vs-combined contrast was not clearly reported in the snippet. — [UNT dissertation PDF](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc5502/m2/1/high_res_d/dissertation.pdf)
- [EXP] Marino (2019), Rutgers dissertation, middle school, compared two harmonic-form instructional sequences and found no significant differences on any outcome. — [Rutgers RUcore](https://rucore.libraries.rutgers.edu/rutgers-lib/60883/)
- [QUAL/theory] Solli, Aksdal & Inderberg (2021) build a "usage-based" theory of learning jazz language through aural imitation, taking up the jazz-as-language analogy that Berliner left open. — [Aksdal PDF](https://erlingak.folk.ntnu.no/LearningJazzLanguage/LearningJazzLanguage.pdf)
- [QUAL] Group playing by ear in higher education: a study of how imitation, invention, and group improvisation develop in formal settings. — [BJME abstract](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-music-education/article/abs/group-playing-by-ear-in-higher-education-the-processes-that-support-imitation-invention-and-group-improvisation/B7E13278E1B86B708C155F107CC6B204)

### Inferences
- For a classically trained pianist, the strongest-supported "first lever" is building **aural imitation** (play/sing back what you hear, transcription-style work). It is the most replicated predictor across May 2003, Madura 1996, and the Michigan thesis, and Woody & Lehmann show it is a specific deficit in classically-only-trained players.
- **Self-evaluation** (recording yourself and judging it accurately) was the top predictor in May 2003. This may mean critical listening skill matters as much as generation. Because the data are correlational, it may also just reflect that better players judge themselves better.
- Theory knowledge is a real predictor (Madura), especially for blues tasks. The data do not support "ear only, no theory." The supported picture is ear plus theory.
- Woody & Lehmann's finding that singing back is easier than playing back points to singing as a low-cost bridge from the ear to the hands.

### Gaps
- Watson 2010's actual results (did the aural group outperform the notated group on achievement or self-efficacy?) are unverified. Get the full text before ranking "learn from recordings vs. from written solos."
- I found no RCT on adult classically trained pianists learning jazz specifically. Almost all samples are wind players or school students.
- I could not confirm Ciorba (2009) as a published predictive model. It may be the U. Miami item above, but treat it as unverified.

## 2. Ethnographic accounts of how jazz musicians actually learned (Berliner, Monson, Sudnow)

### Takeaway
Berliner's ethnography (50+ New York professionals, 1980s) describes an informal apprenticeship: intensive listening to and imitation of recordings, learning from mentors and peers, and jam sessions, compared by musicians to first-language acquisition. This is [QUAL] evidence describing what experts did. It shows the common pathway, not that the pathway is optimal.

### Cited Findings
- [QUAL] Berliner, *Thinking in Jazz* (1994): based on interviews with more than 50 New York musicians, combined with participant observation and musicological analysis. Many learned outside formal schooling, through study groups and jam sessions. Berliner drew on his own experience as a trumpeter. — [New Music USA](https://newmusicusa.org/?p=276022); [Goodreads summary](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/1041617)
- [QUAL] Berliner compares learning jazz by copying recordings to acquiring a first language. Solli, Aksdal & Inderberg note that this is a frequent comparison among jazz musicians, and that Berliner leaves the mechanics open. — [Aksdal PDF](https://erlingak.folk.ntnu.no/LearningJazzLanguage/LearningJazzLanguage.pdf)
- [QUAL] Bjerstedt reads Berliner's accounts as a "stolen knowledge" model: learners absorb material by listening to teachers and records ("apprenticeship at the gramophone"). He notes that aural imitation is now built into academic programs such as Berklee and the New School. — [Bjerstedt, Cambridge (BJME)](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/77013C652DEC5588A5AB3AF990E0BAE8/S0265051716000103a.pdf/stealing-knowledge-in-a-landscape-of-learning-conceptualizations-of-jazz-education.pdf)
- [QUAL] Critique: one commentator argues that Berliner frames jazz musicians as outside academia, even though many interviewees taught or studied in schools. — [Journal of Aesthetic Education PDF](https://scholarlypublishingcollective.org/uip/jae/article-pdf/55/4/82/1902197/jaesteduc.55.4.0082.pdf)

### Inferences
- The ethnographic pathway (listen deeply → imitate recordings by ear → absorb phrases → play with others → get feedback from mentors and peers) lines up with the correlational finding that aural imitation predicts achievement (Section 1). Two independent kinds of evidence point the same way. Together they are a moderately strong case for ear-based imitation of recordings.
- Playing with others (jam sessions, ensembles) is central in the ethnography. No controlled study I found isolates its effect.

### Gaps
- **Monson, *Saying Something* (1996)** and **Sudnow, *Ways of the Hand* (1978)**: I could not retrieve sources this session. From background knowledge (unverified here): Monson stresses interactive, conversational rhythm-section interplay and musical "intermusicality" (quotation and reference). Sudnow is a first-person phenomenological account by a sociologist and classically trained pianist learning jazz piano. He describes moving from cobbling together memorized "licks" to the hand "knowing its way" around the keyboard, and his first breakthrough came from imitating the *pulse and rhythmic shape* of recorded players rather than notes. Sudnow (N=1, autoethnography) is especially relevant to a classical pianist. Verify before citing.
- I found no quantitative survey measuring how often professionals used each learning pathway.

## 3. Cognitive models of improvisation (Pressing, Johnson-Laird, Norgaard, Limb, others) and practice implications

### Takeaway
Corpus analysis (Norgaard on 48 Parker solos) strongly supports heavy use of stored, recombined patterns in expert bebop improvisation. Interview data (Norgaard's 7 artist-level players) show experts mix pattern retrieval with rule- or theory-driven generation. Neuroimaging (Limb & Braun) suggests expert improvisation involves reduced self-monitoring, consistent with automaticity. Together these support building an internalized vocabulary and automatizing it. Neuroimaging, however, does not prescribe practice methods.

### Cited Findings
- [CORPUS] Norgaard (2014), *Music Perception*, "How Jazz Musicians Improvise: The Central Role of Auditory and Motor Patterns." He analyzed 48 Charlie Parker solos by converting pitches to directional intervals. About 82.6% of notes begin a 4-interval pattern that recurs elsewhere in the corpus, and 57.6% begin a recurring interval-*and*-rhythm pattern. The 4-interval pattern at each note recurred on average 26.3 times. Patterns up to 49 intervals long were found. Norgaard concludes this supports the theory that pre-formed structures are inserted during improvisation. Limitation: one player, one style (bebop), and recurrence does not prove stored motor programs. — [Carleton bibliography](https://jazztheory.ssac.carleton.ca/view.php?id=4574); [GSU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/music_facpub/78); [Sonicfield summary](https://labs.sonicfield.org/library/how-jazz-musicians-improvise-2012)
- [CORPUS] Norgaard, Montiel & Spencer used the same corpus to drive a computer improvisation algorithm. Its output contained pattern structures similar to the original. — [GSU ScholarWorks item](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/items/ed865623-bb33-40fb-8f36-0387589ce833)
- [CORPUS] A dissertation found Parker used a greater volume and frequency of patterns than Lee Konitz and Warne Marsh. Pattern reliance varies by player and style. — [UNL Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/dissertations/AAI10247656)
- [CORPUS] Norgaard & Römer (2022), *Jazz Education in Research and Practice*, applied linguistic corpus tools (AntConc) to Parker's solos, extending the jazz-as-language and formulaic-language analogy. — [GSU ScholarWorks item](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/items/b0b305ea-ac42-40aa-be9a-72282e1b27fb)
- [QUAL] Norgaard (2011), JRME 59: 109–127 (dissertation 2008, UT Austin). **Seven** artist-level jazz musicians each recorded a blues solo, then described their thinking while listening back and viewing the notation (retrospective think-aloud). — [UT repository record](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/handle/2152/17893?show=full); [dissertation PDF](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/bitstream/2152/17893/2/norgaardm.pdf)
- [QUAL] Norgaard (2017) used the expert-derived coding frame to compare developing improvisers' thinking against experts. Norgaard, Dunaway & Black compared expert improvisers across cultural traditions. — [CML Lab, UT](https://cml.music.utexas.edu/research/cognition-in-jazz-improvisation); [CiteDrive record](https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/descriptions-of-improvisational-thinking-by-expert-musicians-trained-in-different-cultural-traditions/)
- [Theory/computational] Johnson-Laird (2002), *Music Perception* 19(3): 415–442, "How jazz musicians improvise." Argues that creativity theories should be computable. Chord-sequence generation is memory-heavy and needs working memory or notation of intermediate results, so it is prepared in advance. Real-time melodic improvisation uses algorithms that minimize working-memory load. Supported by analysis and a computer model, not human experiments. — [Sonicfield library](https://sonicfield.org/library/how-jazz-musicians-improvise); [Carleton bibliography](https://jazztheory.ssac.carleton.ca/view.php?id=267)
- [NEURO] Limb & Braun (2008), *PLoS ONE* 3(2): e1679. fMRI with **six** professional jazz pianists comparing improvisation with over-learned sequences, in two paradigms of differing complexity. Improvisation showed widespread deactivation of dorsolateral prefrontal and lateral orbital cortex, focal activation of medial prefrontal (frontal polar) cortex, widespread sensorimotor activation, and limbic deactivation (amygdala, hippocampus). The authors interpret this as reduced self-monitoring and conscious volitional control. Limitations: N = 6, all male, constrained in-scanner keyboard. Press coverage says no single "creative area" was found. — [UCSF Sound Health](https://soundhealth.ucsf.edu/content/neural-substrates-spontaneous-musical-performance-fmri-study-jazz-improvisation); [Diagnostic Imaging](https://www.diagnosticimaging.com/view/functional-mr-reveals-how-brain-bops-during-jazz-improv); [Hearing Review](https://hearingreview.com/hearing-products/accessories/headphones/in-jazz-improv-large-portion-of-brains-prefrontal-region-takes-five-to-let-creativity-flow)
- [Review] Overview of scientific investigations of improvisation (philosophy-of-jazz wiki). — [PhilosophyOfJazz.net](https://philosophyofjazz.net/wiki/Ontimpr5._Scientific_investigations_of_improvisation)

### Inferences
- **Vocabulary/lick learning is well grounded descriptively**: in Parker's corpus most notes begin recurring patterns, and many of those patterns are rhythm-plus-pitch units. This supports learning phrases *with their rhythm* as units, not as pitch sequences alone.
- Johnson-Laird's division of labor (prepare harmony offline, generate melody online with low memory load) implies that harmonic knowledge must be pre-learned and automatized *before* the bandstand. For a pianist, that means voicings and changes under the hands, so that working memory is free for melodic choices.
- Limb & Braun's reduced-monitoring result fits automaticity and flow accounts. It is also consistent with the classical-musician "inner critic" problem (Section 7). Practice implication, offered as inference only: separate low-judgment generation sessions from critical review of recordings, which also matches May's self-evaluation finding.
- The pattern evidence is style-specific (bebop/Parker). Players like Konitz and Marsh used fewer patterns, so formula-heavy practice may suit bebop and blues vocabulary more than freer styles.

### Gaps
- **Pressing (1988) "Improvisation: methods and models"**: not retrievable this session. From background knowledge (unverified): improvisation is a series of event clusters generated using a knowledge base (stored materials and excerpts) and referents (a tune or form). Expertise involves building a large knowledge base and automating motor and perceptual processes, freeing attention for higher-level control. Verify before citing.
- **Limb & Donnay (2014)** ("trading fours" fMRI, language areas active) and later improvisation neuroscience (e.g., Beaty; Lopata; Rosen et al. on expertise and "letting go") were not retrieved.
- **Biasutti & Frezza (2009)** (dimensions of improvisation questionnaire: anticipation, use of repertoire, emotive communication, feedback, flow) and **Barrett** (organizational/improvisation research) were not retrieved.
- No experimental study found that tests whether lick-based instruction beats rule- or theory-based instruction for adult learners.

## 4. Deliberate practice and expertise research; spacing, interleaving, retrieval, mental practice

### Takeaway
Deliberate practice is a substantial but far from sufficient predictor of music performance: about 21% of variance per Macnamara et al. (2014), with a corrected correlation of r ≈ .61 per Platz et al. (2014), and the variance interpretation is contested. Evidence for interleaving in music is mixed and small-N, with modest retention benefits at best. I found no jazz-specific experimental evidence on spacing, retrieval practice, or mental practice.

### Cited Findings
- [Meta-analysis] Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), *Psychological Science*: 88 studies, >11,135 participants. Deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, **21% in music**, 18% in sports, 4% in education, and <1% in professions. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/07/140716123835.htm); [Stafforini summary](https://stafforini.com/works/macnamara-2014-deliberate-practice-and/)
- [Reanalysis] Hambrick et al. (2014), *Intelligence*, "Facing facts about deliberate practice": practice accounted for about one-third of reliable variance in chess and music (30% for music, assuming practice-estimate reliability of .80). — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4101876/)
- [Meta-analysis] Platz et al. (2014), *Frontiers in Psychology*, music only: corrected r = .61 between deliberate practice and music achievement. The authors argue this should not be read as "DP explains 36% of variance." — [Frontiers DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00751)
- [Critique] A 2021 *Current Psychology* paper argues that many analyzed effect sizes did not measure individualized deliberate practice, so practice's explanatory power may be underestimated. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-021-02326-x)
- [EXP, small N] Carter & Grahn (2016), *Frontiers in Psychology*: **10** advanced clarinetists, within-subjects, blocked vs. interleaved (3-minute switches), 12 minutes per piece, rated by 3 professionals. Interleaved scores were consistently higher on average but mostly non-significant. A few rater-specific comparisons were significant (e.g., Rater 3, concerto excerpts, p = .02), and blocked technical studies lost ground from Day 1 to Day 2. Inter-rater correlations ranged from .02 to .59. The authors call the effects "modest." All participants normally practiced blocked. Six of ten found interleaving more useful, though six of ten *preferred* blocked. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989027)
- [EXP] Studies cited by Carter & Grahn: Stambaugh (2009), beginner clarinetists in grades 5–6, interleaved group faster at retention. Stambaugh & Demorest (2010), grade 7, no schedule effect on accuracy. Stambaugh (2011a), university players, effect for woodwinds but not brass. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989027)
- [EXP] Abushanab & Bishara (2013), *Memory & Cognition* 41: 928–937, "Memory and metacognition for piano melodies: Illusory advantages of fixed- over random-order practice." The title indicates that fixed (blocked) practice *feels* better but the advantage is illusory. Details not retrieved. — [Bulletproof Musician (cites it)](https://bulletproofmusician.com/why-the-progress-you-make-in-the-practice-room-seems-to-disappear-overnight-part-2/)
- [EXP, small N] Wong (2022), Western University honors thesis: 21 proficient pianists, blocked vs. interleaved. Not significant, with interleaved slightly better. — [Western Scholarship@Western](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/psychd_uht/42)
- [Unfinished] A Grahn-lab student report (Dong) tested blocked vs. interleaved learning of *piano improvisation styles*. It stated the hypothesis, but I found no results. — [Grahn lab PDF](https://www.jessicagrahn.com/uploads/6/0/8/5/6085172/xinjie-dong-medsci3999b-final-report-.pdf)
- [EXP] A 2020 *Psychology of Music* article on contextual interference in music practice. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735620922595)

### Inferences
- "Hours of deliberate practice" matters but leaves most variance unexplained. Prior ability, aural skill, starting age, and quality of practice matter too. For an advanced classical pianist, transferable technique is an asset, and the *new* variance to address is aural and stylistic, not technical.
- Interleaving (e.g., rotating keys, tunes, or lick variants within a session) has weak but directionally positive support for retention. It also tends to *feel* worse, which is a metacognitive trap per Abushanab & Bishara and the Carter & Grahn preference data. It is low-risk to adopt. Rank it as "probably helps, modest effect."
- Practicing material in all 12 keys and over varied tunes is a form of interleaving and variability. This is supported by general motor-learning principles, not by jazz-specific trials.

### Gaps
- Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993) is the foundational violinist/pianist study. It was not re-retrieved this session but is well known: retrospective practice-hour estimates by skill level, Berlin conservatory.
- No studies found comparing **practice habits of jazz vs. classical musicians** (e.g., time spent playing along with recordings or in informal sessions).
- **Mental practice** in music (e.g., Bernardi et al. 2013 on pianists) and **retrieval practice** or **spacing** in music were not retrieved. I found no jazz-improvisation-specific experiments on these.
- No evidence found on transfer from classical technique to jazz improvisation outcomes.

## 5. Swing feel / microtiming and rhythm as the novice–expert differentiator

### Takeaway
Swing ratio is not a fixed 2:1 "triplet" feel. In Friberg & Sundström's drummer recordings it decreases as tempo rises (roughly from a long-short ratio at slow tempos toward even eighths at fast tempos). Soloists also vary their eighth-note ratios and their timing relative to the beat. This is descriptive [CORPUS/acoustic] evidence on what experts do. I found little direct experimental evidence on *how* swing is learned, or quantitative data showing rhythm as *the* differentiator between novices and experts.

### Cited Findings
- [CORPUS] Friberg & Sundström (2002), *Music Perception* 19(3): 333–349, "Swing ratios and ensemble timing in jazz performance: Evidence for a common rhythmic pattern." Analyzed ride-cymbal timing of four internationally known jazz drummers at about 110–330 bpm. Swing ratio decreased as tempo increased. They proposed a duration threshold (around 100 ms for the short note), which later work treats as an open question. Linear tempo-ratio relationships are disputed by Collier & Collier (1996). — [Honing & de Haas 2008 PDF](https://mcg.uva.nl/mcg-2023/papers/honing-haas-2008.pdf); [Carleton bibliography](https://jazztheory.ssac.carleton.ca/view.php?id=198)
- [Perception EXP] Friberg & Sundström's 1999 listener study: listeners adjusting swing ratio in synthesized performances preferred larger ratios at slower tempos and smaller ratios at faster tempos. — [ASA 137th meeting summary](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/137th/friberg.html)
- [Perception EXP] Honing & de Haas (2008) examined whether swing timing is tempo-invariant, and whether experts can perceive this. — [Honing & de Haas 2008 PDF](https://mcg.uva.nl/mcg-2023/papers/honing-haas-2008.pdf)
- [Perception EXP] Butterfield (2010), *Music Perception*, "Participatory discrepancies and the perception of beats in jazz." Tested whether listeners, including those with little training, can hear the small bass/drum asynchronies (Keil's "participatory discrepancies") claimed to create swing. — [Sonicfield library](https://labs.sonicfield.org/library/participatory-discrepancies-and-the-perception-of-beats-in-jazz); [Columbia J-I-R](https://sites.music.columbia.edu/j-i-r/index-p=280.html)
- [Analytical] Butterfield (2011), *Music Theory Spectrum*, "Why do jazz musicians swing their eighth notes?" Analyzed beat/upbeat ratios of soloists vs. drummers and argued that soloists use small ratio variations expressively. — [Columbia J-I-R](https://sites.music.columbia.edu/j-i-r/index-p=280.html)
- [Analytical] Benadon (2006), *Ethnomusicology*, "Slicing the Beat: Jazz Eighth-Notes as Expressive Microrhythm." Benadon (2007, *MTO*) argues theory relies too much on isochrony and proposes treating swing ratios as part of the note-value repertoire. — [MTO 13.1 Benadon](https://mtosmt.org/classic/mto.07.13.1/mto.07.13.1.benadon.html)
- [Computational] Communications Physics (2022): notes that participatory discrepancies are widely cited as the main source of swing, and calls this "a very strong claim" because it makes microtiming the dominant component. Further methodological details were not retrieved. — [Nature Comms Physics](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42005-022-00995-z)
- [Corpus tool] The "swingogram" (Dittmar et al. 2017/18, JNMR) tracks swing-ratio variation across famous solos (e.g., Freddie Hubbard, Gerry Mulligan). — [AudioLabs Erlangen](https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.com/resources/MIR/2017-JNMR-SwingRatio/GerryMulligan-TheRedDoor-Solo)
- [Historical] Competing early-20th-century conceptions of "swing" as a rhythmic quality. — [Carleton bibliography](https://jazztheory.ssac.carleton.ca/view.php?id=4714)

### Inferences
- Notated "swing = triplet" instruction, which classical readers will default to, is empirically wrong. Expert swing ratio is tempo-dependent and varies by player and context. The supported way to learn it is ear-based: play along with recordings and imitate timing, not just notes. This links back to the aural-imitation evidence in Section 1.
- Because Norgaard found recurring *interval-plus-rhythm* patterns (Section 3), rhythm is embedded in the vocabulary unit itself. That supports transcribing and imitating with exact timing and articulation.
- For a classical pianist, a plausible high-value drill (inference): practice with recordings, a metronome on 2 and 4, or a drum loop, and record and compare your eighth-note feel to a reference player's.

### Gaps
- I found **no controlled study of how swing feel is acquired** (e.g., a training intervention that improves microtiming).
- I found no quantitative study confirming rhythm/time feel as *the* primary differentiator between novice and expert improvisers. This is a widespread pedagogical claim, but it is unverified empirically in what I retrieved. (Background knowledge, unverified: some studies of jazz-ensemble ratings find rhythmic and timing measures distinguish skill levels, and Ashley and others have examined swing perception in experts vs. novices.)
- The full texts of Butterfield's perception results (whether listeners can detect the asynchronies) were not retrieved.

## 6. Singing/audiation (Gordon's Music Learning Theory) and improvisation

### Takeaway
Evidence linking audiation-based instruction to improvisation is mostly from children and small samples. Azzara (1993) is a quasi-experiment with 66 fifth graders that favored improvisation-emphasis instruction. Vocal and aural imitation predicts jazz improvisation (correlational). I found no study directly testing audiation aptitude as a predictor of *adult* jazz improvisation, or testing "sing before you play" as an intervention for adults.

### Cited Findings
- [Quasi-EXP] Azzara (1993), JRME: 66 fifth-grade instrumental students. Students whose curriculum emphasized audiation-based improvisation performed at significantly higher achievement levels than those without the emphasis. The authors call it preliminary. Abstract only. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/3345508)
- [EXP] Audiation-based recorder instruction (50 fourth graders) contributed to beginning instrumental achievement. A separate study of 108 fourth and fifth graders found audiation-inclusive instruction ineffective for error-detection skills. — [SAGE 2009 abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022429409337201); [ProQuest abstract](https://www.proquest.com/docview/305047222/abstract/50EAC07116B54F55PQ/1)
- [Theory] Gordon distinguishes audiation from imitation ("audiation is not imitation") but treats imitation as the readiness for learning to audiate. AMMA was built to measure adults' stabilized audiation. — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED380341.pdf)
- [CORR] Madura (1996): vocal imitative ability predicted vocal jazz improvisation achievement (see Section 1). — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/3345598)
- [CORR/quasi-EXP] Woody & Lehmann (2010): singing melodies back took fewer attempts than playing them back, for all groups. — [Hildebrandt, PMEA summary](https://www.pmea.net/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/Hildebrandt.TeachingEarPlaying.PMEA_.2016.pdf)
- [QUAL/dissertation] Nonnemaker (2017, UGA EdD), Kodály-based high school choral students: vocal improvisation practice improved students' *perceived* sight-singing ability and ability to "hear music internally." Self-report. — [UGA OpenScholar PDF](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/19600/files/nonnemaker_brandon_m_201712_edd.pdf)
- [Pedagogy] Audiation-based improvisation is taught at the Hamburg conservatory (HfMT), indicating institutional uptake. — [HfMT Hamburg](https://www.hfmt-hamburg.de/en/studium/studienorganisation/vorlesungsverzeichnis/ss25-improvisation-im-sinne-des-audiationsbasierten-musiklernens-4544)

### Inferences
- Singing (scat-singing lines, singing along with solos, singing before playing) has indirect support. Singing is the easier channel for ear reproduction (Woody & Lehmann), and vocal imitation predicts improvisation (Madura). It is cheap and plausibly helps a pianist link inner hearing to the keyboard. The direct experimental evidence for adults is weak.
- Gordon's framework, which places imitation before audiation, is consistent with the ethnographic "imitate first" pathway. Gordon's specific sequencing claims are theory, not established findings.

### Gaps
- No adult or classical-pianist study tests "sing-then-play" interventions on improvisation outcomes.
- No study found correlating Gordon's AMMA or audiation scores with jazz improvisation achievement in adults.

## 7. Classically trained vs. jazz-trained musicians: differences and what classical musicians struggle with

### Takeaway
A well-controlled EEG/behavioral study (Bianco et al. 2018, N = 30 professional pianists) shows that genre training leaves distinct planning signatures. Jazz pianists replan faster when harmony is unexpected, while classical pianists are more precise in manner of playing and show more conflict with unexpected harmonies. Qualitative and self-study research identifies the classical musician's barriers as fidelity-to-score attitudes (Werktreue), fear of mistakes, difficulty making musical decisions, and anxiety. Classical-only training also predicts weaker ear-playing (Woody & Lehmann).

### Cited Findings
- [NEURO/behavioral, cross-sectional] Bianco, Novembre, Keller, Villringer & Sammler (2018), *NeuroImage* 169: 383–394. 30 professional pianists (15 jazz-specialized for ≥2 years, 15 classical) imitated chord progressions silently. Jazz pianists reprogrammed earlier when harmonies were unexpected (with beta power decrease) but made more fingering or manner errors. Classical pianists showed greater conflict on incongruent harmonies (increased theta) and executed manner of playing (fingering) more accurately. The authors attribute the differences to genre-specific training ("different enduring markers impressed in the brain"). Press coverage frames this as jazz emphasizing *what* to play (harmonic planning) and classical emphasizing *how* to play it (execution). That framing is a summary, not a measured construct. "Manner" refers to fingering/movement parameters, per the press summary. Limitation: cross-sectional, so self-selection cannot be excluded. — [UCL Discovery (open manuscript)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10043049/); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/jazz-classical-pianist-brains-8324); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/01/180116123713.htm)
- [QUAL, autoethnography and interviews] Ayerst (2021), Sheffield PhD, "Learning to improvise in western classical music." Traced cognitive and emotional barriers to Werktreue (fidelity to the score) and a sense of role as serving every detail of a written score. In his self-study he initially struggled to make musical decisions and take creative ownership. He gained control by building a conceptual grasp of underlying structure and improvising within rule-based systems. — [White Rose eTheses](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/29759/); [Sheffield profile](https://mmm.sites.sheffield.ac.uk/who-we-are/jonathan-ayerst)
- [QUAL, N = 1] Institute of Education (2009) doctoral self-case-study: a trained classical musician learning to improvise over eight weeks found it "frustrating and anxiety provoking." — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10019875)
- [QUAL] Performance anxiety in musical training (four personal histories), PMC 2025. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11848721/)
- [OPINION] A classical-music.com essay describes an internal "panic button" wired to fear of mistakes as common among classically trained players. — [Classical Music](https://www.classical-music.com/features/articles/on-the-classical-musicians-fear-of-improvising/)
- [CORR/quasi-EXP] Woody & Lehmann (2010): formally trained musicians without vernacular experience needed more attempts to play melodies by ear (see Section 1). — [Woody CV](https://arts.unl.edu/faculty/cvs/Bob%20Woody%20June%202013%20CV.pdf)

### Inferences
- The classical pianist's specific deficits, in order of evidence strength: (1) ear-playing and aural imitation (quasi-experimental and correlational); (2) harmonic replanning under uncertainty, i.e., fast "what chord, what next" processing (cross-sectional EEG); (3) psychological barriers such as score fidelity and fear of error (qualitative, small N); (4) swing feel, inferred from the timing literature since no direct study was found.
- Ayerst's self-study suggests classical musicians may benefit from *structured constraints* first (rule-based systems such as a blues scale over a 12-bar form, guide tones, limited note sets) before open improvisation. This is N = 1 and should be treated as a hypothesis.
- Bianco et al. imply that a classical pianist should practice harmony as flexible, re-plannable knowledge (voicings in all keys, ii–V–I's, reharmonization, comping over unexpected changes) rather than as fixed fingered passages.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal study found tracking classical pianists converting to jazz and identifying what predicts success.
- Other jazz-vs-classical comparisons (e.g., Przysinda et al. 2017 on ERP responses to unexpected chords; Vuust et al. on rhythmic MMN in jazz musicians) were not retrieved this session.
- No research found on how much classical sight-reading ability transfers, helps, or interferes with jazz learning, beyond the qualitative claims above.
