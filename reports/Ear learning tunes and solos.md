# Sing Kind of Blue, Then Widen Out

Start with three riff blues heads (**C Jam Blues, Bags' Groove, Freddie Freeloader**). Then take your first solo from the same record that holds Freddie Freeloader: **Miles Davis on *Kind of Blue* (1959)**. His solo runs **2:12 to 4:30, six 12-bar choruses in concert B-flat at about 127 bpm**, and it is the brass solo educators name most often as a first ear target ([allsolos](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/94/miles-davis-trumpet-solo-on-freddie-freeloader); [Learn Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/5-jazz-solos-you-need-to-learn/)). After that, the path runs outward from the blues. First come Miles on "So What" and "All Blues" from the same album. Next is Hank Mobley's "Dig Dis" on *Soul Station*, followed by Wynton Kelly's piano solo on the same track as the bridge back to the keyboard. Then the single-line guitar and vibes blues: Kenny Burrell, Wes Montgomery, and the first chorus of Milt Jackson's "Bags' Groove". After those come Sonny Rollins on "Tenor Madness", Parker's simpler blues, Kenny Dorham on "Blue Bossa", and finally the sung-then-played trumpet solos of Chet Baker. The sources agree on the method: sing the line with the recording, then without it, and only then find it on the instrument. Write nothing down until you can play it from memory ([JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/what-why-where-who-when-and-how-to-transcribe/); [University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)). The evidence is uneven. Educators agree well on *which* tunes and solos come first. Solid timings, keys and tempos exist for only five solos, all from one site (allsolos.com). Most tune keys and tempos below are unverified, and you should confirm them at the keyboard in the first minute of listening. That check is itself ear training. Two errors circulate and should not be repeated. "Blue Bossa" is on **Joe Henderson's** *Page One*, not a Kenny Dorham album. "Tenor Madness" is a 12-bar blues with **no bridge**. This report builds on the earlier ranking that put learning by ear from recordings at #1 for this learner ([Jazz piano highest impact practices](Jazz%20piano%20highest%20impact%20practices.md)). It assumes that context and does not repeat it.

## How to read the verification labels

Every key, tempo, album and start time below has one of three statuses. A detail followed by a linked source is **verified by that source**: the source states it, but nobody in this research pass checked it against the audio. A detail marked **unverified** comes from the researchers' general knowledge. It is probably right, but treat it as a hypothesis to test by ear. A detail marked **conflict** means sources disagree, and the report says which way to lean. The solo timings all come from allsolos.com, because jazztrumpetsolos.com, the other timing database, now redirects to an unrelated site. A single-site dependency is worth knowing about. Several useful recommendations (Trumpet Herald, Sax on the Web, some jazzguitar.be threads) could only be read as search-result snippets, because the pages blocked direct fetching. Those carry less weight, and the report says so where they matter.

Keys are always **concert pitch**, meaning what you hear and what you play on piano. allsolos also lists trumpet keys, which are written a whole step higher than they sound. Ignore those.

## Twenty tunes in five stages, all anchored on the blues

The educator lists converge on a core of riff blues heads. BetterSax ranks six from easiest to hardest by how few notes the melody uses and how literally its phrase repeats ([BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/the-6-easiest-blues-heads-you-must-know/)). Learn Jazz Standards labels a larger set "easy" or "entry level" ([LJS, 28 Blues Heads](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/28-jazz-blues-heads-you-need-to-know/); [LJS, 20 Easy Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)). Utah's blues taxonomy runs from the three-chord "blues blues" to jazz blues with ii-Vs, then minor and modal blues, then the reharmonized Bird blues ([University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)). No source prescribes a single sequence, so the order below is a synthesis. It follows BetterSax's difficulty logic inside the blues, Utah's taxonomy across blues types, and keyboardimprov's tiering beyond the blues ([Keyboardimprov](https://keyboardimprov.com/here-are-the-real-book-tunes-you-should-learn-first/)). It also pulls Freddie Freeloader, So What and All Blues earlier than their raw difficulty would put them, because *Kind of Blue* holds the first three solos you will learn. A head learned just before its solo makes the solo easier to hear.

Stage 1 is the riff blues, where the same phrase repeats and the only harmony is I, IV and V. Stage 2 adds jazz-blues ii-Vs and bebop rhythm. Stage 3 is minor, modal and triple-meter blues. Stage 4 is 16-bar riff and Latin tunes. Stage 5 is the songbook, learned from singers, because, as Utah notes, "vocalists often sing relatively straightforward versions of the head," and the lyrics help you remember the melody ([University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)).

The tunes left out matter too. Satin Doll is a good stretch target once Autumn Leaves feels solid. Its A section moves almost entirely by step, but its bars 5–6 "almost defy analysis" ([JazzStandards.com](https://www.jazzstandards.com/compositions-0/satindoll.htm)). Equinox, Footprints and Cantaloupe Island fit naturally between Stages 3 and 4. Their keys and albums are unverified, and Cantaloupe Island appears on no fetched educator list. **Billie's Bounce, Au Privave, Blues for Alice and Chi Chi** are standard blues heads, but no source calls them easy, so they belong after Now's the Time. Misty, My Funny Valentine, Solar and Stella by Starlight appear on some beginner lists. They are much harder to learn *by ear* than on paper, because recordings take the ballads rubato and heavily embellished and Solar compresses moving key centers into a short form. Defer them. That is an inference from the LJS definition of "intermediate," not a sourced ranking.

There is one pianist-specific refinement. Learn each head from the horn original, which gives the clearest single line. Then listen to a piano version to hear how a pianist voices the same changes in the left hand. *Night Train* covers C Jam Blues, Bags' Groove and Things Ain't in one album ([JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/oscar-peterson-trio-night-train/)). Peterson embellishes his heads, though, so treat his versions as harmony lessons, not melody references.

## Twenty-one first solos, from Miles's fragments to Chet's sung lines

The educator lists overlap most on two clusters. One is **Miles Davis on *Kind of Blue***: Learn Jazz Standards lists Freddie Freeloader first, JazzFuel and a Trumpet Herald thread pick So What, and Jeff Libman's "50 Easy Jazz Solos" lists both ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/5-jazz-solos-you-need-to-learn/); [JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/?p=52678); [Libman PDF](https://cdn.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/50-Easy-Jazz-Solos.20141.pdf)). The other is **Hank Mobley on *Soul Station***, named by JazzAdvice, Libman and Sax on the Web. On the reed side, Lester Young's 1936 "Lady Be Good", Rollins, Dexter Gordon's "Second Balcony Jump" and Parker's "Cool Blues" form a strong second tier. JazzAdvice's eight beginner blues solos supply the guitar and slow-blues picks. Every list routes around the same players. Libman's "easy" list takes the trombone and bass solos from *Blue Train* and the trumpet solos from *Kind of Blue*, skipping Coltrane and Cannonball on both records ([Libman PDF](https://cdn.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/50-Easy-Jazz-Solos.20141.pdf)).

The educators agree on what makes a solo digestible. It is in **4/4 at medium or medium-slow tempo**, on **standard blues changes**, with **clear phrases separated by space**, a mix of rhythmic "statements" and simple eighth-note lines, and a solo you love ([JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/8-awesome-blues-solos-for-beginners-to-transcribe/)). JazzFuel adds mid-register melodicism and closeness to the tune's melody ([JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/?p=52678)). For a learner who sings first, two more filters matter, both inferences: phrases short enough to sing on one breath, and a register you can sing after moving it by an octave. Horn players' breath-shaped phrasing produces both naturally.

The order below is a synthesis; no source ranks these solos against one another. It front-loads blues, then uses each new solo to add one new element: a new instrument, a new feel, or a new harmonic challenge.

Paul Desmond on "Take Five" is on Libman's list and is lyrical over a two-chord vamp. It sits outside the blues and in 5/4, so treat it as a detour rather than a step. No source in this research recommended a specific trombone, clarinet or flute solo as a first target.

### Singers serve as phrasing models more than solo targets

The sources treat vocal recordings differently from horn solos. Three vocal recordings do appear in educators' transcription assignments: Sarah Vaughan's "All of Me" on *Swinging Easy* ([SMT Discuss](https://discuss.societymusictheory.org/discussion/comment/1422)), Ella Fitzgerald's "Smooth Sailing", chosen for its "nice manageable tempo" ([Earl MacDonald](https://www.earlmacdonald.com/jazz-solo-transcription/)), and Nat King Cole's "Love", which a trumpet educator uses because students can finish it "in one sitting" (snippet only) ([Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1716909)). Ella's scat on "How High the Moon" from *Ella in Berlin* (Feb. 13, 1960) runs **eight choruses** ([USFQ](https://repositorio.usfq.edu.ec/bitstream/23000/8870/1/144630.pdf)) and gets "progressively harder" ([SMT Discuss](https://discuss.societymusictheory.org/discussion/comment/1422)). Learn one chorus as a target and listen to the rest.

The most useful vocal tool for this method is vocalese. King Pleasure's "Moody's Mood for Love" (Prestige, 1952; one source says 1954) sets Eddie Jefferson's words to James Moody's 1949 tenor solo, verbalizing it note for note ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Pleasure); [Lewis Porter](https://lewisporter.substack.com/p/vocalese-2-of-3-first-recordings)). The lyrics carry the rhythm of a real bebop solo. No source frames it as a teaching tool, but for a learner who sings first, it works as a mnemonic for an instrumental line.

For melody interpretation, the cited models are the Billie Holiday–Lester Young small-group sides of 1937–38, such as "I Must Have That Man" and "This Year's Kisses" (Jan. 25, 1937) ([Mosaic](https://www.mosaicrecords.com/lester-young-and-billie-holiday/); [Ethan Iverson](https://ethaniverson.com/rhythm-and-blues/a-beginners-guide-to-the-master-takes/)), and Sinatra, who "never sang a song strictly according to the sheet music's rhythms" ([KeyboardImprov](https://keyboardimprov.com/5-things-jazz-pianists-can-learn-from-frank-sinatra/)). Singing a head the way Holiday sang it and then playing it on piano directly corrects the classical habit of playing written rhythms literally. Holiday makes a poor *melody* reference, though: one educator warns she "takes lots of liberties" ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/ljs-podcast/how-do-i-know-the-real-melody-to-a-jazz-standard/)). Learn the plain tune from Sinatra or Ella first, then study her as interpretation.

## Seven blocks that pair each head with its solos

The pairing principle is simple. Learn a head, then a solo on the *same track*, so the form and changes are already in your ear when the improvisation starts. Then, where one exists, add a piano solo or piano version on the same record to bring the line back to the keyboard. *Kind of Blue* and *Soul Station* each hold a whole block on one album. No source gives durations for learning a solo; Learn Jazz Standards says only that the repetition may run into "hundreds" of passes ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/how-to-learn/)). Move on when you can sing and play the current target from memory with the record. Don't move on a schedule.

| Block | Heads (in order) | Solos (in order) | Piano tie-back |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Riff blues | C Jam Blues → Bags' Groove → Freddie Freeloader | **Miles, Freddie Freeloader, choruses 1–2** (from 2:12) | Wynton Kelly's solo, which comes *before* Miles on the track and ends at 2:12 ([allsolos](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/94/miles-davis-trumpet-solo-on-freddie-freeloader)): listen and sing phrases. Its start time and difficulty are unrated. Then Peterson's *Night Train* C Jam Blues and Bags' Groove for left-hand ideas |
| 2. Finish *Kind of Blue* | So What → All Blues | Miles, So What (1:30) → Miles, All Blues (1:45) → Miles, Freddie Freeloader choruses 3–6 | Listen to the piano comping under the solos you already sing: Bill Evans on So What and All Blues, Kelly on Freddie Freeloader (personnel from general knowledge, unverified) |
| 3. *Soul Station* | Sonnymoon for Two → Things Ain't → Dig Dis head | Mobley, Dig Dis | **Wynton Kelly, Dig Dis** (JazzAdvice's pianist pick) |
| 4. Guitar and vibes blues | Blue Monk → Chitlins con Carne head | Burrell, Chitlins con Carne → Wes, D-Natural Blues → Milt Jackson, Bags' Groove chorus 1 | Monk Trio "Blue Monk": the composer's own piano |
| 5. Bebop blues | Tenor Madness → Straight, No Chaser → Now's the Time | Rollins, Tenor Madness → Miles, Trane's Blues → Dorham, Chicago Blues → Ammons, Blues Up and Down → Miles's then Parker's solos on Now's the Time → Parker, Cool Blues | Play each solo's line in the right hand over left-hand shells |
| 6. Minor, Latin, 16-bar | Mr. P.C. (slowed) → Watermelon Man → Song for My Father → Blue Bossa → St. Thomas → Doxy | Dexter, Watermelon Man → Dorham, Blue Bossa → Rollins, St. Thomas (first solo) | Hancock and Silver comping on their own tunes |
| 7. Songbook through singers | Summertime → There Will Never Be Another You and But Not for Me (sung versions) → Autumn Leaves → Lester Leaps In | Chet Baker, But Not for Me → Chet Baker, There Will Never Be Another You → Sweets Edison, Sermonette → Lester Young, Lady Be Good → Sweets Edison, Pennies from Heaven | Bill Evans, *Portrait in Jazz* "Autumn Leaves" |

Two choices in this sequence break from the raw difficulty rankings, and they are worth defending. Freddie Freeloader comes before Sonnymoon for Two, although BetterSax ranks Sonnymoon easier. The Miles solo is the strongest-consensus first solo, and learning its head first is worth the small jump in difficulty. Chet Baker comes last although JazzFuel calls his solo short and singable. His solos run over ii-V songbook changes, and they pay off most once a few heads in that harmonic language are already in your ear.

Keep earlier tunes alive while learning new ones. Learn Jazz Standards advises calling the tune again and again so it doesn't fade ([LJS](https://learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard-and-not-forget-it)). Utah's definition of "knowing" a tune is a good bar for when a block is finished: you can play the melody from memory in time, play the changes, talk through them in roman numerals, and transpose to a few keys on the spot ([University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)). With limited practice time, a workable split (an inference, not sourced) is one new phrase per session plus a few minutes playing two or three earlier heads along with their records. That keeps the review cheap and the new material small.

## The eight-step method, from first listen to all twelve keys

The sources describe the same sequence whether the target is a head or a solo. Utah's rule is the clearest guardrail for a reader-first musician: **no lead sheet "until step 4 or later"** ([University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)). JazzAdvice gives the scope rule. For a first target, "choose a short line, anything under four measures," and "make it 10 times easier than you think you need it to be" ([JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/getting-started-with-transcription/)). A chorus, or even eight bars, is a complete goal ([JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/what-why-where-who-when-and-how-to-transcribe/)).

| Step | What to do | Source | Pianist-specific application |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Listen | Play the track 5–10 times with focused attention. Name the form (12-bar blues? AABA?) and phrase structure before touching the keyboard | [Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf); [LJS](https://learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard-and-not-forget-it) | Count choruses aloud. A 12-bar blues heard as three 4-bar lines is the frame for everything that follows |
| 2. Sing with the record | Loop one phrase and sing it until every note matches, slowed to 50% if needed. "No need to be macho" | [JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/getting-started-with-transcription/); [JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/what-why-where-who-when-and-how-to-transcribe/) | Move tenor or trumpet lines by an octave into your range. That keeps the key, so play-along still works. For heads, also sing the bass roots on beats 1 and 3 ([JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-jazz-standards-with-the-piano/)) |
| 3. Sing without the record | Sing the phrase from memory. A forum poster calls singing with and then without the record the fastest route to memory (anecdotal) | [Cafe Saxophone](https://cafesaxophone.com/threads/what-how-and-why-are-you-transcribing.13137/page-4); [LJS LIST method](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf) | Singing without the record shows whether you have the rhythm or only the pitches. Classical readers usually lose the rhythm first |
| 4. Find it, right hand alone | Play the phrase in the **original key and register**. If 1–3 attempts miss a note, move by half steps methodically | [Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf); [SaxSchoolOnline](https://saxschoolonline.com/articles/how-to-transcribe-for-saxophone/) | Use concert keys. Remember that guitar sounds an octave below written pitch (general knowledge), and play where you hear it |
| 5. Play along | Raise the speed while you can still sing it, then play with the record until timing and accents match. Playing along "holds you accountable to playing exactly what the soloist played" | [LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/how-to-learn/); [JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/getting-started-with-transcription/) | The main risk is evening the swing eighths into classical straight eighths. Copy the soloist's placement, not the beat grid |
| 6. Add left-hand shells | Play simple left-hand voicings under the recording to hear where line notes fall against the changes. "If chords clash, just play the 3rd and the 7th" | [PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/) | Start with one shell on beat 1 of each bar. Then play hands together without the record |
| 7. Analyze, then (optionally) write | Name the chord tone each phrase starts on, its direction, and the concept. Write it down only *after* it is learned, and never as a crutch | [JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/getting-started-with-transcription/); [JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/what-why-where-who-when-and-how-to-transcribe/) | Your reading strength makes early notation tempting. Treat writing as analysis. PianoGroove favors staff paper, then MuseScore ([PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/)) |
| 8. Transpose and apply | Play the phrase in other keys, eventually all 12, and drop it into other blues | [JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/the-3-secrets-to-transcribing-jazz-solos/); [Seabrook](https://www.sussexjazzmag.com/improv-column-terry-seabrooks-jazz-tip-of-the-month-no-13/) | Begin with the blues keys you already know from the tune list (F, B-flat, C, G) |

For heads specifically, JazzAdvice adds a harmonic layer between steps 4 and 7. Find the bass roots, then hear each chord's quality (major, minor, dominant, half-diminished), then the progressions that connect them. Its summary is "if you can't hear it, you can't learn it" ([JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-jazz-standards-with-the-piano/)). Check a lead sheet only at the end, expecting errors: "Just because it's on a piece of paper doesn't mean it's correct" ([LJS](https://learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard-and-not-forget-it)). For songbook tunes, learn the lyrics. That advice traces to Lester Young, who believed the lyrics mattered to his improvising ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/lester-young)).

Piano cannot copy everything a horn does. A guitarist who transcribes for other instruments notes that piano "can't do all the stylistic slides, bends, slurs, and ghost notes" ([jazzguitar.be](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/77772-any-advice-transcribing-other-instruments-besides-guitar.html)). Articulation and groove still have to be learned from the record, because they don't survive on paper ([Seabrook](https://www.sussexjazzmag.com/improv-column-terry-seabrooks-jazz-tip-of-the-month-no-13/)). One workable mapping, which is inference rather than sourced technique: ghost notes become barely sounded touches, scoops become a quick grace note from a half step below, and tongued accents become dynamic accents. A classical pianist's touch control is an asset here.

**Tools.** **Transcribe!** (desktop) is the tool educators name most, for A/B looping and slowing without pitch change. PianoGroove warns that its "Show Note Guesses" feature is "not always perfectly accurate" ([JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/what-why-where-who-when-and-how-to-transcribe/); [PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/)). **Amazing Slow Downer** on iPhone and iPad integrates with Spotify and is "very user friendly" ([PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/)). **Audacity** is a free option ([jazzguitar.be](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/83610-what-do-you-use-transcription.html)). **Moises** and **Anytune** add AI stem separation ([MiniTool](https://moviemaker.minitool.com/jp/news/slow-down-music.html); [StemSplit](https://stemsplit.io/blog/moises-ai-review)). Stem separation helps most on the lo-fi records (Parker 1945, Lester Young 1936–39, Holiday 1937). Separation artifacts can blur the very articulation you are trying to learn, though, so check the stem against the full mix. Avoid apps that display the notes, such as SoundSlice, which PianoGroove calls "counterintuitive to the task at hand" ([PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/)). Treat slowing down as scaffolding, not a home. PianoGroove's stated aim is to pick material up "without slowing it down" ([PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/transcription-introduction/)), and a jazzguitar.be thread argues for trying full speed first and slowing only the segment that resists ([jazzguitar.be](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/improvisation/94714-transcribing-jazz-solos-tips-tricks-recommended-recordings.html)). Pitch-shifting a vocal into your singing range helps at step 2. Shift by an octave, or the line will no longer match the record at step 5.

## Simple-looking targets that hide one hard element

JazzAdvice's blanket warning applies: "there's no such thing as an easy solo to transcribe" ([JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/8-awesome-blues-solos-for-beginners-to-transcribe/)). The useful skill is predicting *which* element will be hard, so you can isolate it.

**Rhythm hides behind easy pitches.** Blue Monk's melody is simple but its rhythm is "syncopated and complex" ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)). Now's the Time has easy notes, and its rhythm "will be the biggest challenge" ([BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/the-6-easiest-blues-heads-you-must-know/)). Straight, No Chaser displaces one motif around the bar line, so you will hear it in the wrong place until you count it (inference). Classical training will not warn you about any of these, because the notes look trivial on paper.

**One spot in the harmony hides inside an easy tune.** Satin Doll's bars 5–6 ([JazzStandards.com](https://www.jazzstandards.com/compositions-0/satindoll.htm)), the chromatic tritone-sub descent near the end of Autumn Leaves ([JazzStandards.com](https://www.jazzstandards.com/compositions-0/autumnleaves.htm)), Blue Bossa's key change and Fly Me to the Moon's "non-diatonic surprises" ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)) each break an otherwise diatonic tune. Getz's "Desafinado" runs a 68-bar form whose harmony "many Real Book charts miss" ([JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/?p=52678)).

**Tempo, length and meter.** Mr. P.C. is "fairly fast" ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)). Sweets Edison's "Pennies from Heaven" is spare but moves at **180 bpm** ([allsolos](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/2373/harry-sweets-edison-trumpet-solo-on-pennies-from-heaven)). Freddie Freeloader is hard only because of its **six choruses**, which is why the plan learns two at a time. All Blues has a triple feel that hides the downbeat, and allsolos itself calls it both 6/8 and 6/4 ([allsolos](https://www.allsolos.com/solo/102/miles-davis-trumpet-solo-on-all-blues)). Rollins's "Blue 7" is built from simple material but runs 11:17, and Gunther Schuller's famous essay analyzes it for its long-range motivic structure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxophone_Colossus); [Jazz Studies Online](https://www.jazzstudiesonline.org/node/246)). Treat it as a listening and singing study, not a transcription.

**Ballads.** A transcription-class handout calls ballads deceptively hard to hear, and advises medium tempos and swing-era solos instead ([Scribd handout](https://es.scribd.com/document/248817216/How-to-Transcribe)). Coleman Hawkins's "Body and Soul" is contested. One forum poster calls it a non-complex start ([SOTW](https://www.saxontheweb.net/threads/easy-first-transcriptions.157134/)), while a Michigan State thesis finds that Hawkins's ballad solos lack the stock formulas that make lines easy to isolate ([MSU](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/30662/FULL_TEXT/view)). Side with the thesis and defer it. Misty and My Funny Valentine belong in the same deferred group.

**Players the lists route around.** Coltrane's double-time on "Tenor Madness" ([JazzAdvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/8-awesome-blues-solos-for-beginners-to-transcribe/)) and his "Impressions" ([JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/?p=52678)) are explicitly hard. JazzFuel suggests "My Shining Hour" as an easier Coltrane entry, which Learn Jazz Standards still calls "a little bit more advanced" than Freddie Freeloader ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/5-jazz-solos-you-need-to-learn/)). Cannonball Adderley is contested. Libman lists two of his solos as easy, while a Sax on the Web poster warns he is rough for a first attempt ([Libman PDF](https://cdn.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/50-Easy-Jazz-Solos.20141.pdf); [SOTW](https://www.saxontheweb.net/threads/easy-first-transcriptions.157134/)). Leave his and Coltrane's *Kind of Blue* solos for later. Freddie Hubbard is "tougher" than Dorham (snippet only) ([Trumpet Herald](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1716909)). Milt Jackson's "Bags' Groove" sounds relaxed, but Hal Leonard rates the full solo intermediate, so stop after chorus 1 at first.

**Recordings that are hard to hear.** Sinatra's timbre is "too smooth" for one forum poster to pick out pitches (snippet only) ([jazzguitar.be](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/106798-transcribing-vocalists.html)). The 1927–1945 recordings (Armstrong's Hot Five, Lester Young, early Parker) bury the bass. Pair them with a later, clearer version for the harmony, and keep the original for phrasing. Sonnymoon for Two's pianoless trio gives you no chords at all, only bass and horn. The last two points are inference.

## Errors and conflicts to stop repeating

Several mistakes and disagreements turned up in the sources. They are collected here so they don't leak into practice notes or future reports.

Most tune keys and tempos remain unverified, and nearly every solo start time outside *Kind of Blue*, *Page One* and *Pres & Sweets* is missing. The solos without start times include Dig Dis, Kelly's Freddie Freeloader and Dig Dis solos, Chitlins con Carne, D-Natural Blues, Bags' Groove, Tenor Madness, Cool Blues, Trane's Blues, Chicago Blues, St. Thomas, Watermelon Man and both Chet Baker solos. The Weimar Jazzomat database has a record for Milt Jackson's "Bags' Groove" ([Jazzomat](https://jazzomat.hfm-weimar.de/dbformat/synopsis/solo329.html)) and is the next place to look. Finding the key and the start time yourself takes a minute per track, and that minute is ear training.

## Conclusion

The research changes the problem more than it answers it. "Which solo first" is nearly settled: educators converge on Miles Davis on *Kind of Blue*, and the album's three solos, their heads and Wynton Kelly's comping make it a complete first curriculum on one record. What the sources leave open is *pacing*. No one reports how long a first solo takes, and the only firm guidance is to shrink the target until it feels too easy: four bars, one chorus. For an adult with limited time, the scope rule matters more than the solo list. Two choruses of Freddie Freeloader, sung and played from memory with the record, are worth more than six choruses read from a transcription.

The second takeaway is that the learner's plan of singing first, horns first and blues first turns out to be what the educator lists already imply, even though no source states it for pianists. The lists favor breath-shaped, mid-register, space-filled lines over standard changes, which describes horn and guitar blues solos. They also route consistently around the dense players (Coltrane, Cannonball, Hubbard) even on the records where those players appear. The open risk is not choosing the wrong solo. It is the classical reflex to notate early and straighten the swing eighths. The method guards against both by keeping the score out until the line is already in the ear and under the hands.
